The opening paragraph, page 20

“Well-behaved women seldom make history; against Antinomians and witches, these pious matrons have had little chance at all.”

The famous version drops the second half — where Ulrich's real meaning lives. She wasn't urging women to misbehave; she was lamenting that ordinary, dutiful women left almost no trace in the historical record.

You've probably seen it credited to someone else

Because it spread on T-shirts and mugs — often with no attribution — this line gets handed to whichever famous woman feels right. It belongs to none of them:

✕
Marilyn Monroe
A 2002 quote book printed it under her name. She died in 1962 — fourteen years before the sentence was written.
✕
Eleanor Roosevelt
The most common misattribution online. Fact-checkers have found no such line anywhere in her writings.
✕
Anne Boleyn · “Anonymous”
Occasionally credited to a Tudor queen, or simply left unsigned — which is how it drifted away from its author in the first place.
✓ And it's often misquoted, too. Ulrich wrote “seldom.” The viral “rarely make history” was introduced by journalist Kay Mills, who used the line as a book epigraph in 1995 — the moment it escaped into pop culture.
💡

Why it mattered

Ulrich was a graduate student when she wrote the line — not as a slogan, but as the opening move of a scholarly argument. Women's history, she observed, had fixated on a handful of exceptional or rebellious figures while the vast majority of ordinary, conforming women went unstudied, precisely because they left few dramatic records.

It was a call to recover the lives of the obscure — the opposite of a call to misbehave.

She would go on to model exactly that recovery: her 1990 book A Midwife's Tale, reconstructing the life of an ordinary Maine midwife from her diary, won the Pulitzer Prize. The irony is that her one line about the forgotten was itself lifted out of context, re-worded, and re-assigned — becoming one of the most quoted, mis-quoted, and mis-attributed sentences in American feminism. In 2007 she reclaimed it, writing a whole book to explain what it really meant.

About the author

LU

Laurel Thatcher Ulrich

Born July 11, 1938, Sugar City, Idaho · Historian of early America

A pioneering historian of women's lives, Ulrich won the 1991 Pulitzer Prize and the Bancroft Prize for A Midwife's Tale, and was named a MacArthur Fellow in 1992. She is a University Professor Emerita at Harvard — and coined her most famous line as a graduate student.
