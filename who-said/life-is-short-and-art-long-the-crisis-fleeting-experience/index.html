Who really said it

Life is short, and Art long; the crisis fleeting; experience perilous, and decision difficult

Attributed to Hippocrates c. 460 – c. 370 BC · Greek physician of Kos, the “Father of Medicine”
≈ Attribution status: Attributed ✓Reuse status: Free to reuse

This is a real, traceable sentence: it is the opening aphorism of the Aphorisms, one of the treatises of the Hippocratic Corpus. But this exact English is the wording carried by the standard online texts — Wikisource ↗ and the Internet Classics Archive — which credit Francis Adams’s ↗ 1849 translation even though his printed 1849 volume reads “Life is short, and the Art long; the occasion fleeting; experience fallacious, and judgment difficult.” And the byline itself cannot be verified: none of the texts in the Corpus ↗ is proven to be by Hippocrates himself.

Life is short, and Art long; the crisis fleeting; experience perilous, and decision difficult. The physician must not only be prepared to do what is right himself, but also to make the patient, the attendants, and externals cooperate. Aphorisms I.1

The second sentence is almost always dropped, and it is the one that fixes the meaning: this is a rule for practising physicians, not a reflection on art and mortality. Note also that this is the online-text wording: Adams’s printed 1849 volume reads “Life is short, and the Art long; the occasion fleeting; experience fallacious, and judgment difficult.”

How we checked

  • The wording checks out against the standard English text. The Internet Classics Archive’s Aphorisms, Section I ↗ opens with the sentence verbatim, and the title page for the text ↗ credits it as “Aphorisms · By Hippocrates · Written 400 B.C.E · Translated by Francis Adams.” That translator credit, though, is not borne out by Adams’s own printed volume — see below.
  • Wikiquote ↗ carries the line in its sourced Aphorisms section at 1:1 — not among the disputed entries — alongside the Greek, Ὁ βίος βραχὺς, ἡ δὲ τέχνη μακρὴ, ὁ δὲ καιρὸς ὀξὺς, ἡ δὲ πεῖρα σφαλερὴ, ἡ δὲ κρίσις χαλεπή, and a variant English translation. Quote Investigator has no article on this quotation.
  • The 1849 print does not match the wording on this page. The Internet Archive’s scan ↗ of The Genuine Works of Hippocrates, vol. 2 records Francis Adams (1796–1861) as translator, printed for the Sydenham Society, London, 1849 — and sets Aphorism I.1, on p. 697, as “Life is short, and the Art long; the occasion fleeting; experience fallacious, and judgment difficult.” Adams’s own footnote on that page glosses “The occasion is fleeting,” so the reading is not a scanning artefact, and Wikisource’s aphorism 2 matches the print verbatim — the substitution is at I.1 alone. The scan therefore fixes the work, the translator and the date; it does not support this page’s wording.
  • The one thing that cannot be verified is the byline. Wikipedia’s survey of the Hippocratic Corpus ↗ states flatly that “of the texts in the corpus, none is proven to be by Hippocrates himself,” and notes that Franz Zacharias Ermerins identified at least nineteen authorial hands in the collection.

Fact-check

What people get wrong about it

Nothing here is a fabrication — the sentence is genuinely the first line of the Aphorisms. But almost every part of how it gets quoted is off by one step: the words are not quite the Victorian translator’s they are credited to, the “art” is not art, the author is a collective, and one of the most-quoted versions is a different poet’s line entirely.

~
The English wordingCredited to Francis Adams — but not his printed text translation

The English you are quoting circulates under Adams’s name in every standard online text, but it is not what he printed. His 1849 volume, at p. 697 ↗, reads “Life is short, and the Art long; the occasion fleeting; experience fallacious, and judgment difficult” — four differences from the popular line — and his own footnote there glosses “The occasion is fleeting.” The Greek reads Ὁ βίος βραχὺς, ἡ δὲ τέχνη μακρὴ, ὁ δὲ καιρὸς ὀξὺς…, and Adams was a Scottish physician who practised in Banchory, Aberdeenshire ↗ and translated Greek medical texts on the side. Wikiquote records a third rendering again: “Art is long; life is short; opportunity is fleeting; judgement is difficult; experience is deceitful.”

~
The word “Art”Greek τέχνη — craft, not fine art misread

The original term tékhnē means technique and craft, not “art” in the aesthetic sense ↗ — here, specifically, the art of medicine. The aphorism’s own next sentence settles it by turning immediately to what the physician must do with the patient and the attendants.

~
The authorAn unidentified writer of the Hippocratic Corpus authorship

The Aphorisms is classed among the “possibly genuine” works, but “the question of whether Hippocrates himself was the author of any of the treatises in the corpus has not been conclusively answered” ↗. One scholar identified at least nineteen hands across the collection.

✓
The look-alike lineHenry Wadsworth Longfellow different poem

“Art is long, and Time is fleeting, / And our hearts, though stout and brave, / Still, like muffled drums, are beating / Funeral marches to the grave” is Longfellow’s “A Psalm of Life,” first published in 1838 ↗. It echoes the aphorism, but it is Longfellow’s own English and easy to mistake for the Hippocratic line.

✓ The sentence really is the first line of the Hippocratic Aphorisms, and it has carried Hippocrates’s name since antiquity — but the English you are quoting is a variant circulating under Francis Adams’s name, not the wording he printed in 1849 ↗, the “Art” is medicine, and no text in the Corpus has ever been proven to be Hippocrates’s own work.

Context

Why this line has travelled so far

Read the whole aphorism and the meaning snaps into focus. The sentence does not stop at “decision difficult” — it continues: “The physician must not only be prepared to do what is right himself, but also to make the patient, the attendants, and externals cooperate.” This is not a meditation on mortality or on the permanence of art. It is a working doctor’s warning, set at the head of a manual of clinical rules: the craft takes longer to master than you have, the moment to act passes quickly, the evidence of experience misleads, and you will still have to decide.

The line then acquired a second life in translation. It is best known in Latin as Ars longa, vita brevis — art first, life second, which inverts the Greek, where life comes first. Geoffrey Chaucer opened the Parlement of Foules in the 1380s with “The lyf so short, the craft so long to lerne ↗,” still using “craft” for the Greek τέχνη. By the time Henry Wadsworth Longfellow wrote “Art is long, and Time is fleeting ↗” in 1838, “art” had drifted toward the modern aesthetic sense, and the aphorism had quietly become a poem about legacy.

“Art” here means medicine. The craft takes longer to learn than a life takes to spend.

Why the Latin reads backwards — and why it matters

The Greek begins with the short thing: Ὁ βίος βραχὺς, ἡ δὲ τέχνη μακρὴ — life is short, but the craft is long. Wikipedia notes that the standard Latin version reverses that order ↗, and the tag that stuck in common use, Ars longa, vita brevis, leads with art.

The inversion is not merely cosmetic. Hippocrates’s clause order makes the brevity of a life the premise and the length of the craft the problem that follows from it. Put art first and the sentence starts to sound like a claim about art outlasting the artist — which is roughly the meaning most people now attach to it, and roughly the opposite of a clinical caution about how little time a physician has to get good.

The traditional author

Who Hippocrates actually was

H

Hippocrates

c. 460 – c. 370 BC · Greek physician of Kos, the “Father of Medicine”

Hippocrates was born on the Aegean island of Kos around 460 BC and is traditionally called the Father of Medicine for separating medical practice from superstition and priestcraft. The catch is that almost nothing about him is firm: Wikipedia’s survey notes that “very little is known concretely about what Hippocrates himself thought, wrote and did, because his achievements were often conflated with those of the practitioners of Hippocratic medicine and the writers of the Hippocratic Corpus.” The roughly sixty treatises that bear his name were written by many hands — one nineteenth-century scholar counted at least nineteen — and whether Hippocrates wrote any of them has never been conclusively settled.
