You cannot shake hands with a clenched fist

Spoken by Indira Gandhi 1917–1984 · Prime Minister of India
✓ Attribution status: Verified

Documented: she used the line at a New Delhi press conference on 19 October 1971, amid the crisis that became the India–Pakistan war, and it was reported the next day in The New York Times. What she did not do was coin it — the phrase was already a stock American saying by the 1950s.

The paper trail

  • Indira Gandhi used the line at a New Delhi press conference on 19 October 1971, reported the next day in The New York Times and logged in Wikiquote’s sourced section ↗.
  • But it wasn’t hers to coin: a 27 August 1956 Los Angeles Times report of a Presbyterian sermon by a Rev. Dr. Hjerpe already ran “one cannot shake hands with a clenched fist,” per Barry Popik’s citation file ↗.
  • Earlier still, a 1953 Omaha newspaper advertisement ran “You Can’t Shake Hands / With a closed fist” — also documented by Popik ↗.
  • Online it is now widely mis-pinned on the other Gandhi: Goodreads files it under Mahatma Gandhi ↗.

Short proverbial sayings like this one aren’t protected by copyright, and this one has no single author — versions circulated in American newspapers from at least 1953. Reproduce it freely; just don’t credit it as Indira Gandhi’s original coinage.

Fact-check

Who it’s wrongly tied to

Two errors travel with this line: it is often pinned on the wrong Gandhi, and even for the right one it is mis-framed as an original coinage.

✕
Incorrectly attributed to Mahatma Gandhi common

There is no record of Mohandas (Mahatma) Gandhi saying or writing this; the words surface in Indira Gandhi’s 1971 remarks and in 1950s American newspapers, not in his writings. The shared surname (they were not related) fuels the mix-up, yet Goodreads still lists it under Mahatma Gandhi ↗.

✕
Miscredited as coined by Indira Gandhi

She genuinely said it in October 1971, but she was repeating a stock saying rather than inventing one: it appears in U.S. papers through the 1950s and ’60s, the earliest near-exact wording being a 27 August 1956 Los Angeles Times sermon report, as Barry Popik documents ↗.

✓ Indira Gandhi did say the words in 1971 — that much is documented — but the phrase was an anonymous proverb already circulating in American newspapers since at least 1953, so she popularized it rather than originated it ↗.

Context

Why she said it

When Indira Gandhi delivered the line in October 1971, India and Pakistan were sliding toward war. Millions of refugees were pouring across the border from East Pakistan — soon to become Bangladesh — and by December the two nations were fighting outright. At her press conference she used the clenched-fist image to insist that India wanted peace but could not negotiate with an adversary braced to strike.

The line landed because it was memorable and instantly quotable, and Gandhi’s stature carried it around the world. That is exactly how a recycled newspaper proverb becomes ‘a Gandhi quote’: a famous speaker gives an anonymous saying a name and a date.

You cannot shake hands with a clenched fist.

Read the full story

The image is older and homelier than the diplomacy it now decorates. Barry Popik’s citation file traces close variants through mid-century American print — a 1953 Omaha advertisement (“You Can’t Shake Hands / With a closed fist”), a 1956 Los Angeles Times report of a Presbyterian sermon using “one cannot shake hands with a clenched fist,” and further 1950s–60s appearances — all predating Gandhi’s 1971 remark.

None of those earlier uses is a clear single ‘origin’ either; the phrase reads as a floating proverb that many speakers reached for independently. Gandhi’s contribution was to attach it to a globally reported moment, which is why her name, not a 1950s minister’s, is the one it now carries.

About Indira Gandhi

IG

Indira Gandhi

1917–1984 · Prime Minister of India

Indira Gandhi (1917–1984) was India’s third prime minister and the only woman to hold the office, serving 1966–1977 and again from 1980 until her assassination in 1984. A commanding and polarizing leader, she took India through the 1971 war that created Bangladesh and later imposed the controversial 1975–77 Emergency. She was the daughter of India’s first prime minister, Jawaharlal Nehru — and, despite the surname, no relation to Mahatma Gandhi.
