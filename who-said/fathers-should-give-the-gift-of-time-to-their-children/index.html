Who really said it

Fathers Should Give the Gift of Time to Their Children

Not Ann Landers — an anonymous poem she reprinted Unknown — an anonymous Father’s Day poem In print by June 1956 · author never identified
? Attribution status: Disputed ?Reuse status: Rights unconfirmed

Two things to untangle here. First, this sentence is not a quotation — it is the headline of Quote Investigator’s article about the verse ↗, written by the researcher, not by anybody being quoted. Second, the verse itself is anonymous: it was running in American newspapers around Father’s Day by 6 June 1956, under the title “Give Him a Day”, and QI found it labelled anonymous in every 1956–57 printing it located. Ann Landers reprinted it in her syndicated column on 11 June 1988 — sent in by a reader, with no author name given — which is how her byline came to be attached to it.

What shall you give to one small boy? A glamorous game, a tinseled toy, A barlow knife, a puzzle pack, A train that runs on curving track? A picture book; a real live pet. No, there’s plenty of time for such things yet. Give him a day for his very own— Just one small boy and his dad alone. A walk in the woods, a romp in the park, A fishing trip from dawn to dark. Give the gift that only you can— The companionship of his Old Man. Games are outgrown, and toys decay— But he’ll never forget if you “Give him a day.”

The 1956 Oxnard Press-Courier text, as transcribed by Quote Investigator ↗. The 1988 version Ann Landers printed differs in small ways — “barlow knife” had become “Boy Scout knife” — which is the ordinary drift of a poem passed hand to hand for three decades.

How we verified this

  • Quote Investigator’s investigation ↗ locates the earliest printing in the Oxnard Press-Courier of 6 June 1956, page 1, and records that “the author was anonymous”. Its conclusion is explicit: “The author was unidentified in all the cites located by QI.”
  • The same article lists three further newspaper printings in the following months — the Logansport Press (Indiana) in 1956, the Millbrook Round Table (New York) in April 1957 and the Augusta Chronicle (Georgia) in June 1957 — noting that “each time the poem was labeled anonymous”.
  • For the Ann Landers connection, QI cites her column of 11 June 1988 in The Spokesman-Review / Spokane Chronicle, page C2, under the title “In lieu of toys, give son some time”, where she introduces the verse as a reader’s submission and gives no author name.
  • The column that carried it in 1988 was written by Esther “Eppie” Lederer ↗, who had used the Ann Landers pen name since 1955; the column itself ↗ was created by Ruth Crowley in 1943. Neither wrote the poem.

Fact-check

Often misattributed

This one is misattributed twice over: a research headline gets read as a quotation, and a column that reprinted a poem gets read as having written it.

~
The credit in circulationAnn Landers Popularizer, not author

Her 1988 column is where most readers met the verse, and she wrote “I hope every father who reads it will want to keep this copy” — the words of someone passing something on. QI records that the poem reached her from a reader and carried no author name ↗, and that it had already been in newspapers for thirty-two years.

✕
The sentence itself“Fathers should give the gift of time to their children” Not a quotation — a research headline

These words appear in no newspaper, no column and no poem. They are the title Quote Investigator gave its own article, in the site’s house style of capitalising headlines. Quoting them as though someone said them, and then attaching a speaker, manufactures a quotation out of a bibliography entry.

✕
The real authorUnknown No source

Four separate newspapers printed the poem in 1956 and 1957 and not one named a writer. QI located no attribution in any citation. The honest answer to “who wrote this?” is that nobody knows.

~
The wording“Boy Scout knife” Wording drift

The 1956 text has a “barlow knife”; by 1988 it was a “Boy Scout knife”, and a 1994 anthology had replaced the closing couplet with “Give the best gift to that bright lad— The companionship of his dear dad.” An anonymous poem with no copyright owner to object drifts freely.

✓ The verse is an anonymous American Father’s Day poem, “Give Him a Day”, in print by 6 June 1956. Ann Landers reprinted it in 1988 and named no author, because she had none to name — as Quote Investigator documents ↗.

Context

Why it mattered

The poem is a small piece of mid-century newspaper filler that worked: a list of presents a father might buy, each rejected, ending on the one thing he cannot buy. Papers ran it in the week before Father’s Day because it fitted a column inch and said the thing everybody wanted said, and readers clipped it out, which is why it kept reappearing.

Clipping is also how it lost its author. A poem that travels by scissors and envelope arrives with no byline attached, and the last person to print it inherits the credit. Ann Landers was simply the most famous link in a chain that had been running for three decades.

Give him a day for his very own—just one small boy and his dad alone.

Read the full story

The reader who wrote to Quote Investigator remembered the poem from an Ann Landers column and misremembered one line, recalling a “tinker toy” where the 1956 text has a “tinseled toy”. That is a fair illustration of how the verse survived: in people’s memories and in cuttings, not in any book of record.

Worth separating the two claims this page handles, because they fail differently. “Ann Landers wrote this” is false and can be shown false. “Fathers should give the gift of time to their children” is not false, it is simply not a quotation — it is a description of a poem, written in 2012 by a researcher, which has since been passed around inside quotation marks.

About Ann Landers

AL

Ann Landers

Pen name, created 1943 · American newspaper advice column

Ann Landers was never one person. The pen name was created by the Chicago Sun-Times columnist Ruth Crowley in 1943 and taken over in 1955 by Esther “Eppie” Lederer (1918–2002), who wrote it for forty-seven years to a readership of some ninety million. A column that ran every day lived on what readers mailed in — poems, jokes, homilies, mostly unsigned — and reprinting them under a famous byline is how a great many anonymous pieces, this poem among them, ended up credited to her.
