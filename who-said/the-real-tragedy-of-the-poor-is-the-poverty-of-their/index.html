Who really said it

The real tragedy of the poor is the poverty of their aspirations

Not Adam Smith — usually credited to Ernest Bevin Ernest Bevin 1881–1951 · British trade union leader, Minister of Labour & Foreign Secretary
? Attribution status: Disputed ?Reuse status: Rights unconfirmed

Adam Smith did not write this. The word ‘aspiration’ does not occur anywhere in The Wealth of Nations or The Theory of Moral Sentiments, and the Adam Smith Institute states flatly that he never said it. The line is usually re-credited to the Labour politician Ernest Bevin — but that attribution is itself undocumented: no speech, book, or dated source has been located, and Bevin’s own Wikiquote page does not list it.

Adam Smith Institute: “Adam Smith never said that” ↗

How we verified this

  • The simplest test first: search Smith’s own books. The word ‘aspiration’ does not appear a single time in the complete text of The Wealth of Nations ↗, nor in The Essays of Adam Smith ↗, the volume carrying The Theory of Moral Sentiments. Neither book contains the phrase ‘tragedy of the poor’.
  • Smith’s own institute says the same thing in plainer words. Sam Bowman, writing for the Adam Smith Institute ↗, states that Smith “never said” it, “as some people who have never read him think”, and that it is hard to think of a less Smithian view — Smith having had deep compassion for people unlucky in life.
  • The trail then points at Ernest Bevin, and stops. The LSE’s British Politics and Policy blog ↗ records only that New Labour in the 2000s “rediscovered the expression ‘poverty of aspiration’ – whose origin has been attributed to Labour politician Ernest Bevin”. Attributed by whom, when, and where is not said.
  • And the Bevin credit does not survive checking either: the line is missing from Bevin’s sourced Wikiquote page ↗ and from his Wikipedia biography, while the quote site that assigns it to him ↗ supplies no speech, book, or date. Quote Investigator has not examined this quote at all.

No rights status can honestly be asserted, because no source work has been identified. The line is not from Adam Smith, so his pre-1931 public-domain works do not cover it; and if it does trace to Ernest Bevin (d. 1951) or to some unidentified twentieth-century writer, the containing work could well still be in copyright. Treat the wording as of unknown origin and unknown status rather than assuming it is free to reuse.

Context

Why this one keeps landing on Adam Smith

The quote has the shape of an economist’s aphorism — a crisp inversion, a diagnosis of poverty that locates the problem inside the poor rather than around them. Adam Smith is the default signature for anything that sounds like economics and reads like a maxim, which is exactly how a line with no traceable origin ends up under the most famous name in the field.

The irony is that the sentiment cuts against Smith. He wrote that no society can be flourishing and happy where the greater part of its members are poor and miserable, and his account of poverty is structural, not a scolding of the poor for wanting too little. The Adam Smith Institute — hardly a hostile witness — calls the line the opposite of Smith’s voice and puts it down to people who have never read him.

It is hard to think of a less Smithian view.

The Bevin correction — and why it isn’t the end of the story

The usual fix, repeated on quote sites and in comment threads, is that the real author is Ernest Bevin. There is a genuine thread here: the phrase ‘poverty of aspiration’ does have a Bevin association in British political memory, and New Labour revived the expression in the 2000s to explain educational outcomes among working-class children.

But an association is not a citation. The LSE’s own account says only that the phrase’s origin has been attributed to Bevin — passive, unsourced, and about the phrase rather than this sentence. No Hansard record, speech text, or biography has been produced for these words, and Bevin’s Wikiquote page does not include them. Correcting a fake Smith quote with an undocumented Bevin quote just moves the problem.

The name the correction points to

So who was Ernest Bevin?

EB

Ernest Bevin

1881–1951 · British trade unionist, wartime Minister of Labour & post-war Foreign Secretary

The seventh child of Diana Bevin — a Somerset domestic servant and village midwife who called herself a widow after her husband walked out four years before Ernest was born — Bevin never knew who his father was. He left school at eleven, drove a lorry in Bristol, and built the Transport and General Workers’ Union into Britain’s most powerful labour organisation, serving as its General Secretary from 1922. Churchill made him Minister of Labour in 1940; Attlee made him Foreign Secretary in 1945. He is the figure most often named as the origin of the phrase ‘poverty of aspiration’ — a coinage New Labour revived in the 2000s — though no primary source for these exact words has ever been produced, and the credit may be as loose as the Adam Smith one it was meant to correct.
