Who really said it

Fairy tales are more than true: not because they tell us that dragons exist, but because they tell us that dragons can be beaten

Not G.K. Chesterton — actually Neil Gaiman Neil Gaiman born 1960 · English author of Coraline and The Sandman
? Attribution status: Disputed

This exact wording is Neil Gaiman’s, written as the epigraph to his 2002 novel Coraline. Gaiman credited it to Chesterton, but later admitted he wrote his own paraphrase and, after ten years, forgot it wasn’t Chesterton’s original — whose actual line in ‘The Red Angel’ (1909) says dragons can be killed, not that fairy tales are ‘more than true.’

Fairy tales are more than true: not because they tell us that dragons exist, but because they tell us that dragons can be beaten. ↑ Gaiman’s paraphrase — not a verbatim Chesterton quote

Chesterton’s actual passage in ‘The Red Angel’ reads: “The baby has known the dragon intimately ever since he had an imagination. What the fairy tale provides for him is a St. George to kill the dragon … Fairy tales do not give the child his first idea of bogey. What fairy tales give the child is his first clear idea of the possible defeat of bogey.” The words ‘more than true’ and ‘beaten’ are Gaiman’s.

Provenance trail

  • 1909 — Chesterton publishes ‘The Red Angel’ in Tremendous Trifles, arguing fairy tales give children “the possible defeat of bogey” and a “St. George to kill the dragon” (verbatim text ↗).
  • 2002 — Neil Gaiman prints his own paraphrase — “more than true … dragons can be beaten” — as the epigraph to Coraline, crediting Chesterton (as catalogued ↗).
  • Later — Gaiman admits the wording is his, not Chesterton’s: he wrote it before owning the book and forgot over ten years (his statement ↗).
  • The exact wording has never been found in any Chesterton text, only the underlying idea (First Things ↗).

The specific wording is Neil Gaiman’s, first published in Coraline (2002) and under copyright. Chesterton’s underlying 1909 passage in Tremendous Trifles is public domain, but it is worded differently and is not this quote.

Fact-check

Why everyone credits Chesterton

The line is almost universally attributed to G.K. Chesterton — on quote sites, posters, and even the TV show Criminal Minds. The attribution is understandable but wrong: the words are Neil Gaiman’s.

✕
Incorrectly attributed to G.K. Chesterton

The exact sentence appears nowhere in Chesterton’s work. His genuine passage in ‘The Red Angel’ (Tremendous Trifles, 1909) speaks of a “St. George to kill the dragon” and “the possible defeat of bogey” — never “more than true” or “beaten” (First Things ↗).

✕
Sometimes traced through Terry Pratchett rare

A 1994 Pratchett essay paraphrased the same Chesterton idea (“fairy stories tell children that dragons can be killed”), which may have shaped later memory of the line, but Pratchett’s wording is not this quote either (chronology ↗).

✓ The words are Neil Gaiman’s, written as the epigraph to Coraline (2002) as his own paraphrase of Chesterton — a fact Gaiman himself has confirmed ↗.

Context

A misquote its own author created

This is a rare case where the misattribution was set in motion by the very person who wrote the line. When Neil Gaiman began Coraline in the early 1990s, he wanted a Chesterton epigraph but didn’t own Tremendous Trifles — so he wrote out his own version of the passage from memory, planning to check it later. A decade passed before the book was finished, and by then he had forgotten the words were his paraphrase, not Chesterton’s.

Chesterton’s real argument, in ‘The Red Angel,’ is that children already know dragons (fears) exist; what fairy tales add is the news that dragons can be defeated. Gaiman’s crisper “more than true … dragons can be beaten” distills that idea so memorably that it has all but replaced the original.

Gaiman wrote his own paraphrase, credited Chesterton, and then forgot the words were his.

The true author of the wording

Neil Gaiman

NG

Neil Gaiman

born 1960 · English author

Neil Gaiman (b. 1960) is an English author of fantasy fiction, comics, and screenplays, best known for The Sandman, American Gods, Stardust, and the children’s novel Coraline (2002). He used the ‘dragons can be beaten’ line as that book’s epigraph, attributing it to G.K. Chesterton. Gaiman has since explained that he wrote his own paraphrase of a Chesterton passage intending to check the wording later, and forgot over the years that the words were his, not Chesterton’s.
