Who really said it

An investment in knowledge pays the best interest

Not Benjamin Franklin — an unverified saying first seen in the 1840s Unknown (19th-century American press) True origin undocumented · first printed 1848–1849, decades after Franklin died in 1790
? Attribution status: Disputed

There is no documentary evidence Franklin ever wrote or said this. The line surfaces only in Boston newspapers of 1848–49 — nearly 60 years after his death — already credited to “Dr. Franklin,” and it appears nowhere in Yale’s Papers of Benjamin Franklin.

If a man empties his purse into his head, no man can take it away from him. An investment in knowledge always pays the best interest. ↑ the modern meme keeps only the second sentence and drops “always”

This fuller two-sentence form is how the saying actually circulated in 1840s–50s newspapers, already misattributed to “Dr. Franklin.” The popular one-liner is a later trimming of it.

The Big Apple: “An investment in knowledge always pays the best interest” (Barry Popik) ↗

Tracing the wording

  • Etymology researcher Barry Popik documents that the saying first appears in the Daily Evening Transcript of Boston in 1848–49 ↗, already credited to “Dr. Franklin” — roughly 58 years after Franklin’s death.
  • Popik notes the phrase “appears nowhere, in whole or in part, in Yale’s Papers of Benjamin Franklin ↗,” the definitive scholarly edition of everything he is known to have written.
  • The Library of Congress reference work Respectfully Quoted flags the line as “Unverified” ↗, attributed to Poor Richard but never located in it.
  • It is also absent from the sourced-quotations list on the Wikiquote page for Benjamin Franklin ↗.

The wording dates to the 1840s and is long in the public domain. There is no identifiable author to credit — it is an anonymous 19th-century saying that attached itself to Franklin’s name.

Fact-check

The misattribution

This is one of the most-shared “Franklin” quotes on the internet, printed on classroom posters, library walls and finance blogs. The attribution is almost certainly wrong.

✕
Incorrectly attributed to Benjamin Franklin

No primary source connects the line to Franklin. The earliest known appearance is an 1848–49 Boston newspaper ↗, well after his 1790 death, and the phrase is not in Yale’s Franklin Papers; the Library of Congress calls it “Unverified.”

✕
Sometimes sourced to Poor Richard’s Almanack / The Way to Wealth common

Blogs routinely name Poor Richard or The Way to Wealth as the source, but the words appear in neither work ↗; the attribution to Poor Richard is exactly what the Library of Congress marked unverified.

✓ Bottom line: this is an anonymous mid-19th-century saying ↗ that was pinned on Franklin from the very start — a paraphrase of his known praise for education, not his documented words.

Context

Why it sticks to Franklin

Franklin built his fame on exactly this kind of sentence: short, shrewd, money-minded aphorisms from Poor Richard’s Almanack. So a maxim about knowledge “paying interest” sounds so plausibly his that few readers ever question it — a textbook case of a quote gravitating to the most famous name it could plausibly belong to.

The idea does echo a genuine Franklin theme — he prized education and self-improvement — but echoing someone’s values is not the same as quoting them. The specific banking metaphor of knowledge yielding “the best interest” is the invention of an anonymous 1840s writer, not Franklin’s pen.

It sounds like Franklin because he taught us to expect sentences like this — which is exactly why it isn’t safe to assume it’s his.

Popularly credited author

The man it gets pinned on

BF

Benjamin Franklin

1706–1790 · Founding Father, printer and author

Benjamin Franklin (1706–1790) was a printer, scientist, diplomat and Founding Father whose Poor Richard’s Almanack made him early America’s great coiner of thrift-and-industry maxims. That reputation makes him a magnet for pithy money-and-wisdom sayings he never actually wrote — and this is one of them. The genuine Franklin praised education, but the specific epigram “an investment in knowledge pays the best interest” does not appear anywhere in his verified writings.
