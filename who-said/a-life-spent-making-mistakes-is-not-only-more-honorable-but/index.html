Who really said it

A life spent making mistakes is not only more honorable, but more useful than a life spent doing nothing

Written by George Bernard Shaw 1856–1950 · Irish playwright, critic & Nobel laureate
✓ Attribution status: Verified

Shaw wrote this in the “Preface on Doctors” to his play The Doctor’s Dilemma, published by Brentano’s in 1911. It is confirmed verbatim in the full text of the preface ↗ — though Shaw’s original reads “a life spent in making mistakes.”

Attention and activity lead to mistakes as well as to successes; but a life spent in making mistakes is not only more honorable but more useful than a life spent doing nothing. The one lesson that comes out of all our theorizing and experimenting is that there is only one really scientific progressive method; and that is the method of trial and error. ↑ the popular version drops the “in” and adds a comma

The sentence sits in Shaw’s argument that even a democracy’s clumsy meddling beats passive neglect — his case for trial and error as the only genuinely progressive method.

Fact-check

Correctly credited — but usually misquoted

The attribution to Shaw is sound. What circulates online is a lightly altered wording, not a wrong author.

✕
Commonly misquoted as “A life spent making mistakes…” wording

Popular quote sites render it without Shaw’s word “in” and add a comma after “honorable.” Shaw’s original text ↗ reads “a life spent in making mistakes is not only more honorable but more useful than a life spent doing nothing.”

✓ Bottom line: the words are genuinely Shaw’s, from his 1911 Preface on Doctors ↗ — just quote them with the “in.”

Context

Why he wrote it

The sentence is not a standalone aphorism but a step in an argument. Shaw is defending public scrutiny of the medical profession, insisting that even a democracy’s clumsy interference is better than leaving doctors to police themselves.

“Attention and activity lead to mistakes as well as to successes,” he grants — but the alternative, doing nothing, is worse. From this he pivots to his real claim: that trial and error is “the only really scientific progressive method.”

There is only one really scientific progressive method; and that is the method of trial and error.

About George Bernard Shaw

GBS

George Bernard Shaw

1856–1950 · Irish playwright, critic & Nobel laureate

George Bernard Shaw was an Irish playwright, critic and polemicist whose plays — among them Pygmalion, Man and Superman and Saint Joan — made him the most significant British dramatist since Shakespeare. He was awarded the 1925 Nobel Prize in Literature. His plays typically carry long, argumentative prefaces; the one to The Doctor’s Dilemma is a broadside against the medical profession in which this sentence appears.
