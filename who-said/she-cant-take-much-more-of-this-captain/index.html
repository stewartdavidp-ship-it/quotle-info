Who really said it

She can't take much more of this, Captain!

Not quite — the actual line is Spock’s: “They can’t take much more of this.” Norman Spinrad b. 15 September 1940 · American science-fiction novelist · credited writer, “The Doomsday Machine” (1967)
? Attribution status: Disputed ©Reuse status: In copyright

Scotty never said it. Wikiquote’s Misattributed section ↗ for Star Trek: The Original Series records that neither Montgomery Scott nor anyone else spoke this line during the series. The closest real dialogue is Spock’s, in “The Doomsday Machine” (first aired 20 October 1967, teleplay credited to Norman Spinrad): “Deflector shields at full power. They can’t take much more of this.” The popular version swaps Spock for Scotty, the shields (“they”) for the ship (“she”), and adds a “Captain” who is not in the scene.

“They” is the deflector shields — not “she,” the ship. And there is no “Captain” in the line: Spock is reporting to Commodore Decker, who has taken command, while Kirk is away aboard the derelict Constellation. Every distinctive element of the popular quotation — the speaker, the pronoun, the rank — is absent from the real one.

Fact-check

What the popular version gets wrong

This is a catchphrase that outgrew its source. It is quoted, printed on merchandise and parodied as Scotty’s signature line — but it belongs to the same family as “Beam me up, Scotty”: a line everybody remembers and nobody ever said. Three separate things are wrong with it.

✕
The speakerMontgomery Scott (“Scotty”), played by James Doohan never said it

Wikiquote’s Misattributed section ↗ for the series states that “neither he nor anyone else ever used that famous (and oft-parodied) line.” The line that exists is Spock’s. Searching the complete transcript archive ↗ for all 79 episodes and the ten features surfaces exactly one occurrence of “take much more of this” — and Scotty is not the one saying it.

✕
The wording“She” — meaning the Enterprise drift

In the real line, “they” is not the ship: it is the deflector shields. Spock’s full report in “The Doomsday Machine” ↗ is “Deflector shields at full power. They can’t take much more of this.” The popular version also appends “Captain” — but Spock is reporting to Commodore Decker, who has seized the conn, while Kirk is aboard the derelict Constellation.

✕
The nearest thing Scotty really says“Who Mourns for Adonais?” (1967) closest real line

Wikiquote names this as Scotty’s nearest equivalent: as Apollo’s giant hand crushes the ship, he reports “It’s becoming critical, Captain. We can’t handle it.” The episode transcript ↗ renders it “Becoming critical, Captain. We can’t handle it.” Right character, right panic, different sentence.

✕
The sibling misquote“Beam me up, Scotty” same pattern

The same Wikiquote section ↗ records that this, too, was never said during the original series — the closest being “Scotty… beam us up” in “The Gamesters of Triskelion.” Both misquotes attach to Scotty, and both smooth real dialogue into something easier to remember.

✓ What is actually said, on the bridge while Commodore Decker drives the Enterprise at the planet-killer, is “Deflector shields at full power. They can’t take much more of this” ↗ — and Spock says it. Scotty is not even in the room: he is aboard the wrecked Constellation with Kirk, splicing warp-drive control circuits.

Context

Why this one stuck

Scotty’s function on Star Trek was to stand between Kirk’s ambition and the laws of physics, and the writers gave him a steady supply of dialogue to that effect — warnings, refusals, repair estimates that Kirk then halved. None of it is the sentence the culture kept. What survived instead is a composite: a Scottish burr, a ship in distress, and a “Captain” tacked on the end.

Misquotes of this kind are less failures of memory than of compression. The public remembers a character’s job and invents a sentence tidy enough to carry it — which is why the remembered line is almost always cleaner than anything actually filmed. The real line is a flat technical status report about deflector shields, delivered by the least excitable officer aboard. The catchphrase is what that report sounds like after decades of parody.

A line everyone remembers — from an episode in which the man they credit for it is on another ship entirely.

Did the line originate somewhere else — a novel, an earlier draft?

Origination is the question that usually rescues a misquote: famous screen lines are often borrowed from the source novel and merely sharpened by the film. Not here. The honest answer is that the popular sentence originates nowhere traceable — it is not a tightened version of an earlier line, and it does not appear in any produced Star Trek episode or film, in any speaker’s mouth, in any form.

The episode it is most often traced to does have an origin story of its own, but it is not the line’s. Spinrad built “The Doomsday Machine” out of an unsold novelette; he told StarTrek.com ↗ he had “an early novelette, which wasn’t very good,” resembling both the Doomsday Machine story and Moby Dick. That novelette is the source of the episode’s premise, not of this dialogue.

One caution on authorship. In the same interview Spinrad notes the produced dialogue differed from his script — some was adjusted because William Windom rather than Robert Ryan was cast, and William Shatner had Spock lines trimmed during filming. The on-screen credit is Spinrad’s and he is the author of record; whether these exact words left his typewriter is not something the available record establishes, and this page does not claim it.

The credited writer

Who actually wrote the real line

NS

Norman Spinrad

b. 1940 · American science-fiction novelist, essayist & Star Trek teleplay writer

Norman Spinrad (born 15 September 1940) is an American science-fiction author, essayist and critic, known for novels that earned Hugo and Nebula nominations and the Prix Apollo. He was 27 when he wrote the teleplay for Star Trek’s “The Doomsday Machine” (1967), which he built from an unsold novelette and consciously modelled on Moby-Dick; the episode was nominated for a Hugo Award. He is the author of record for the real line — though, as he has said himself, some dialogue was altered during production, so which of the episode’s exact words are his is not something the record settles.
