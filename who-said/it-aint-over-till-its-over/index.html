Who really said it

It ain't over till it's over

Attributed to Yogi Berra 1925–2015 · Yankees catcher, Mets manager, accidental aphorist
≈ Attribution status: Attributed ©Reuse status: In copyright

Berra is credited with this line everywhere, and he printed it under his own name in The Yogi Book: I Really Didn’t Say Everything I Said! (Workman, 1998), dating it to the New York Mets’ 1973 pennant race — but no 1973 source has been found containing that wording. Etymologist Barry Popik, whose dated chronology is the standard reference here, writes plainly that “This exact quote hasn’t been found”; what the New York Post actually printed on 4 September 1973 was Berra saying “Yeah, it’s running out, but you’re still not out of it until it’s automatic.”

Yeah, it’s running out, but you’re still not out of it until it’s automatic. 4 September 1973 · New York Post

Berra’s answer when asked whether time was running out on the Mets, transcribed by Barry Popik from the New York Post of 4 September 1973, p. 64, col. 1 — the same citation he first posted to the American Dialect Society list in 1999. ADS-L, 10 October 1999 ↗ Popik gives the headline it answered as “Time Running Out on Mets”; Pitcher List renders the same headline more loosely as “Time is Running Out on the Mets.” Pitcher List ↗ This is the closest thing to the famous line in the 1973 record — and it is not the famous line.

Barry Popik: the dated citation chronology ↗

Paper trail

  • 1892 — the sentiment is already a stock proverb with no owner, and not a specifically baseball one: the Lawrence (KS) Daily Journal, 11 April 1892, p. 2, has “THERE is an old adage that says a game is never out till it is played out. This applies as well to politics as to seven up.” Popik notes that no particular person has been credited for the adage. The Big Apple ↗
  • 4 September 1973 — what Berra is actually on record saying during the Mets’ run, in the New York Post, p. 64, col. 1: “Yeah, it’s running out, but you’re still not out of it until it’s automatic.” Popik on ADS-L ↗
  • 1975, then 1984 — the wording tightens in print while his name stays attached. Cincinnati Enquirer, 15 June 1975, p. 1-C: “in the immortal words of Yogi Berra, ‘The game’s not over ’til it’s over.’” Sports Illustrated, 2 April 1984: he knows a managing job, like a ball game, “ain’t over ’til it’s over.” The Big Apple ↗
  • 1998 — Berra prints the polished line himself, in The Yogi Book: I Really Didn’t Say Everything I Said! (Workman Publishing), tied to the 1973 Mets. It is the source Wikiquote gives — though the reference books that predate it, the Random House Dictionary of Popular Proverbs and Sayings (1996) and the American Heritage Dictionary of American Quotations (1997), already date the line to the 1973 Mets without citing any printed source at all; the latter notes that quotations popularly pinned on Berra usually come “without much evidence.” Internet Archive ↗

Fact-check

Right man, tidied-up words

Nobody argues this belongs to someone else. The problem is narrower and more common than a misattribution: the sentence everyone quotes is smoother than anything anyone recorded Berra saying at the time.

✕
The wordingYogi Berra, 1973 Not in the 1973 record

Barry Popik’s chronology puts it plainly: “This exact quote hasn’t been found, but Berra did say in September 1973…” — and what follows is the New York Post of 4 September 1973, p. 64, col. 1: “Yeah, it’s running out, but you’re still not out of it until it’s automatic.” The Big Apple ↗

✕
The phrasing was in the airRed Holzman, New York Knicks coach Same autumn, different man

Popik logs the Asbury Park (NJ) Evening Press of 20 October 1973, p. 11: “The Knicks learned that, as coach Red Holzman phrased it, ‘that a ball game isn’t over until it’s over.’” That is not a claim Holzman coined anything — it is evidence that the construction was circulating in 1973 sports pages beyond Berra. The Big Apple ↗

✕
The exact sentence in printNewspaper writers of the 1980s Attributed, not transcribed

Pitcher List’s fact-check points to the New York Times “Connecticut Journal” column of 30 May 1982, which quotes the future senator Joe Lieberman saying of the attorney general’s race “As Yogi Berra said, ‘it ain’t over till it’s over’” — the earliest instance of the exact wording it could pin down, and expressly only “at least in the New York Times” rather than in print generally. Even that is someone quoting Berra rather than a record of him speaking. Pitcher List ↗

✕
The self-attributionBerra himself, 1998 Claimed in his own book

Berra printed the line in The Yogi Book: I Really Didn’t Say Everything I Said! (Workman Publishing, 1998) and tied it to his 1973 Mets. Internet Archive ↗ Wikiquote accordingly files the line in its sourced “Yogiisms” section, sourced to that book and nothing earlier. Wikiquote ↗

✓ The safest true sentence: the thought is Berra’s and he claimed the line in print himself, but the famous wording is not documented from 1973 — the contemporaneous record is a rougher sentence in the New York Post of 4 September 1973, and the sentiment itself was already a stock proverb by 1892, applied as readily to politics and card games as to baseball. The Big Apple ↗

Context

The comeback that made the line worth saying

Berra took over the Mets in 1972 and by midsummer 1973 was managing a last-place team. Wikipedia’s account of the season has the Mets trailing the Chicago Cubs by 9½ games in the National League East in July; they went on to win the division and reach the World Series. Wikipedia ↗ That is the summer the line is always dated to, and it is why it stuck: the manager who said the season wasn’t finished turned out to be right.

What survives from that September, though, is rougher than the version on the poster. Popik’s chronology records the New York Post of 4 September 1973, p. 64, with Berra answering a question about time running out: “Yeah, it’s running out, but you’re still not out of it until it’s automatic.” Same thought, none of the polish. The Big Apple ↗

“This exact quote hasn’t been found.” — Barry Popik

The sentiment is older than Berra

The underlying adage predates him by generations. Popik’s earliest citation is the Lawrence (KS) Daily Journal of 11 April 1892, p. 2: “a game is never out till it is played out.” He notes that no particular person has been credited with the adage. The Big Apple ↗

The tightening happens across the following decade of newsprint, and Berra’s name rides along with it. Popik logs the Cincinnati Enquirer of 15 June 1975, p. 1-C — “Or, in the immortal words of Yogi Berra, ‘The game’s not over ’til it’s over’” — the New York Times of 23 August 1979, p. B11 with “It’s never over until it’s over,” and Sports Illustrated of 2 April 1984, which has Berra knowing that a managing job, like a ball game, “ain’t over ’til it’s over.” The Big Apple ↗

The man credited

Who he was

YB

Yogi Berra

1925–2015 · Yankees catcher, three-time AL MVP, Mets manager

Lawrence Peter “Yogi” Berra caught for the New York Yankees from 1946 to 1963, won ten World Series titles as a player — more than anyone in major-league history — took three American League MVP awards (1951, 1954, 1955), made 18 All-Star teams, and entered the Hall of Fame in 1972. He then managed: the Yankees in 1964, the Mets from 1972 to 1975, and the Yankees again in 1984–85. He became at least as famous for his looping, self-cancelling one-liners, and was unusually honest about how slippery the record was — he titled his own 1998 collection I Really Didn’t Say Everything I Said!
