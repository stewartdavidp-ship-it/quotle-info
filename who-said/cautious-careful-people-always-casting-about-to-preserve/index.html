Who really said it

Cautious, careful people always casting about to preserve their reputation can never effect a reform

Written by Susan B. Anthony 1820–1906 · American suffragist & social reformer
✓ Attribution status: Verified

Documented as Anthony’s own words by her authorized biographer, Ida Husted Harper, who quotes the passage from the 1860 divorce-law-reform fight — introducing it “Miss Anthony wrote at this time…” (Life and Work of Susan B. Anthony, Vol. I, p. 197). Her documented sentence ends “…never can bring about a reform”; the short version shown here (“…can never effect a reform”) is a lightly compressed paraphrase of it.

Popular wording

Cautious, careful people, always casting about to preserve their reputation and social standing, never can bring about a reform. Those who are really in earnest must be willing to be anything or nothing in the world’s estimation, and publicly and privately, in season and out, avow their sympathy with despised and persecuted ideas and their advocates, and bear the consequences.

This is the passage exactly as Anthony’s authorized biographer records it (Harper, Vol. I, p. 197), from the 1860 divorce-reform fight. The short one-liner shown at the top of this page (“…can never effect a reform”) is a lightly compressed paraphrase of its first sentence.

Fact-check

How to read this attribution

The quote is genuinely Anthony’s — her authorized biographer records it in her own words. Two smaller things are commonly imprecise: the exact wording, and the “speech” title aggregators attach to it.

✕
Exact wordingA modern paraphrase reworded

Harper records “…always casting about to preserve their reputation and social standing, never can bring about a reform.” The popular one-liner drops “and social standing” and swaps in “can never effect a reform” — a tidier restatement of her documented sentence (Harper, p. 197 ↗).

✕
Cited sourceQuotation aggregators mislabeled

Quote sites file the passage under a speech title, “On the Campaign for Divorce Law Reform” (1860) — e.g. WIST ↗. Harper, though, introduces it as something Anthony wrote at the time (Vol. I, p. 197 ↗), not a titled address; the speech label is the aggregators’, not the record’s.

✓ The words are Anthony’s, documented by her authorized biographer in the 1860 divorce-reform context; only the popular one-line phrasing is a compressed paraphrase. There is no Quote Investigator ↗ ruling on it, but Harper settles the substance.

Context

Why it is tied to 1860

At the May 1860 National Woman’s Rights Convention, Elizabeth Cady Stanton introduced resolutions arguing that women should be able to obtain divorce for cause. The proposal split the movement: even the abolitionist ally Wendell Phillips tried to strike the resolutions from the record, and Anthony stood with Stanton in defending them.

This passage belongs to that fight — a defense of taking unpopular positions rather than protecting one’s standing. The sentiment fits Anthony precisely, and her authorized biographer records her writing it during exactly this episode.

“…willing to be anything or nothing in the world’s estimation…”

About the credited author

Susan B. Anthony

Susan B. Anthony

1820–1906 · American suffragist & social reformer

Susan B. Anthony (1820–1906) was a Quaker-raised abolitionist, temperance worker, and the organizational engine of the American woman-suffrage movement, working for decades alongside Elizabeth Cady Stanton. In 1860 the two pushed the National Woman’s Rights Convention to take up divorce-law reform — a stance so controversial that even allies like Wendell Phillips recoiled, the context to which this quotation is usually tied. Anthony’s own words survive mainly in her diaries, letters, the newspaper The Revolution, and Ida Husted Harper’s authorized biography.
