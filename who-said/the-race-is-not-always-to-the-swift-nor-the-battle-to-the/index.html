Who really said it

The Race Is Not Always to the Swift, Nor the Battle to the Strong; But That Is the Best Way to Bet

Not Damon Runyon — actually Hugh E. Keough Hugh E. Keough 1864–1912 · Chicago sportswriter, “In the Wake of the News”
? Attribution status: Disputed ✓Reuse status: Free to reuse

Keough signed himself HEK in the Chicago Tribune, and the line appears as a page epigram in “By Hek” in the Wake of the News ↗, the collection his friend Hugh S. Fullerton assembled after his death in 1912, in the form “The race is not always to the swift, but that is where to look.” The betting wording is credited to him by Franklin P. Adams in Collier’s in February 1919. Runyon, Adams, Grantland Rice and Burns Mantle all used the joke; every one of them named Keough.

The race is not always to the swift, but that is where to look. "By Hek" in the Wake of the News, 1912

That is the 1912 wording, read directly from the Internet Archive’s scan of the Library of Congress’s copy ↗ of Keough’s collection, where it stands as an epigram at the head of a page (page twelve, per Quote Investigator ↗). The familiar betting form — “nor the battle to the strong; but that is the way to bet” — is the one Franklin P. Adams attributed to Keough in Collier’s in 1919. The word best, in the version most often quoted today, belongs to neither of those printings.

How we verified this

  • We read the primary book. “By Hek” in the Wake of the News ↗ (Chicago, 1912), compiled and edited by Hugh S. Fullerton and catalogued at the Internet Archive as scanned from a Library of Congress copy, carries a pithy line at the head of each page. One of them reads, in full: “The race is not always to the swift, but that is where to look.”
  • Quote Investigator ↗ traces the betting wording to Franklin P. Adams in Collier’s: The National Weekly of 8 February 1919, page 9 — where Adams does not claim it: “As Hugh Keough used to say: ‘The race is not always to the swift, nor the battle to the strong; but that is the way to bet.’”
  • Runyon disclaimed it twice. QI records a 1939 Runyon column quoting the “where to look” adage out of a well-thumbed Keough volume, and a 1945 column in which Runyon complains about Bartlett’s giving the line to Adams: “only I think Hughy Keough said it first … when Bartlett’s prints something, I want it accurate because Bartlett’s is one of my favorite sources of thievery and I like to know who I am stealing from.”
  • Wikipedia’s article on Hugh Keough ↗ (24 January 1864 – 9 June 1912) credits him with the quip in exactly the wording this page carries, and confirms he wrote the Chicago Tribune’s “In the Wake of the News” column under the monogram HEK. It cites Quote Investigator for the attribution, so it is a second telling of the same research rather than independent evidence.
  • The joke is a rejoinder to scripture: Ecclesiastes 9:11 in the King James Version ↗ reads “the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong … but time and chance happeneth to them all.” QI shows the wisecrack forming against it from 1833 onwards.

Fact-check

Often misattributed

This is an unusually well-behaved misattribution: every famous writer who used the line said whose it was. The credit drifted anyway, because reference books recorded the messenger.

~
The popular creditDamon Runyon Quoter, not author

Runyon quoted the adage in a 1939 column explicitly about how good Keough’s writing was, reading it out of a Keough volume he owned. Edmund Fuller’s Thesaurus of Epigrams then printed it under Runyon’s name in 1943, and it stuck. Wikiquote’s Runyon page ↗ does not carry it at all.

~
The other creditFranklin P. Adams Popularizer, not author

Bartlett’s Familiar Quotations gave Adams a version of the line — and Runyon wrote a whole column in 1945 to complain, as QI records ↗: “only I think Hughy Keough said it first.” Adams himself had credited Keough in both 1916 and 1919.

~
Also credited it to KeoughGrantland Rice and Burns Mantle Early user

Rice ascribed the saying to “Hek” in June 1919 and the drama critic Burns Mantle to “Hughie Keogh” in 1924 — misspelling the name, but pointing at the right man.

~
The wording“the best way to bet” Later variant

The documented 1919 form is “but that is the way to bet”, and the 1912 form is “but that is where to look.” The superlative is a later smoothing, though it is now the commonest version and the one Wikipedia ↗ prints.

~
The originalEcclesiastes 9:11 Ancestor

The straight line is scripture — “the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong” ↗ — and people had been contradicting it in print since at least 1833, when Blackwood’s Edinburgh Magazine offered “ninety-nine times in a hundred”. Keough’s contribution was the punchline, not the premise.

✓ The line is Hugh E. Keough’s. Runyon said so himself, in print, twice — see Quote Investigator ↗ — and the earliest printing sits in his own 1912 collection ↗.

Context

Why it mattered

Keough was a racing man before he was a columnist, and the joke is a bettor’s joke: scripture says the race is not to the swift, and he agrees — while pointing out that you still have to put money on something. It is the whole argument between faith and odds, in one sentence.

What makes the misattribution worth a page is that nobody committed it deliberately. Runyon, Adams, Rice and Mantle each quoted Keough by name. The credit shifted only when a reference book copied the quotation without the attribution around it.

Every famous writer who used the line said whose it was. The reference books lost the footnote.

Read the full story

Keough died in June 1912 at forty-eight, after six weeks of pneumonia. Hugh S. Fullerton put together “By Hek” in the Wake of the News that December, a small book for the writer’s friends, with one of his lines at the top of every page. That is the artefact almost everything downstream rests on: Runyon was still carrying a copy in 1939, and described it as “well-thumbed”.

The full chain Quote Investigator assembles runs from an 1833 Blackwood’s squib through an 1861 newspaper miscellany, a Wanamaker’s shoe advertisement in 1899 and a threshing-machine trade journal in 1907, to a banner across the Chicago Tribune’s sports page on 2 March 1912 — over a column signed H. E. K. That banner and the December book are the two 1912 citations on which the attribution to Keough rests.

The author

About Hugh E. Keough

HK

Hugh E. Keough

1864–1912 · Chicago sportswriter and racing official

Hugh Edmund Keough (24 January 1864 – 9 June 1912) was born in Hamilton, Canada West, started in newspapers at seventeen and worked at them for thirty-one years, ending as the Chicago Tribune’s sports columnist, where he wrote In the Wake of the News and signed off “HEK”. He spent part of his career as an official at horse-racing tracks across the Midwest and South, which is audible in almost everything he wrote. He died of pneumonia in Chicago at forty-eight; friends printed a small collection of his lines the same year, and sportswriters were still quoting them decades later.
