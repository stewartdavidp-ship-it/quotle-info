The line is credited to Picasso on virtually every quote site, but it is pinned to no speech, letter, book, or interview. The 1935 “Conversation avec Picasso” that some point to does not contain it — the words ‘imagine’ and ‘imagination’ appear nowhere in that text — and it is absent from the standard sourced collections of his words. How we checked it

  • No Quote Investigator article treats this phrase, and Wikiquote’s Picasso page does not list it in its Sourced, Attributed, or Misattributed sections — the specialists have not tied it to him.
  • The source people gesture at — the 1935 “Conversation avec Picasso” (Cahiers d’Art; translated in Alfred Barr’s Picasso: Fifty Years of His Art) — is reproduced in full on Wikiquote and in an Art in Theory anthology text, and neither contains the word ‘imagine.’
  • Every aggregator that carries it — Goodreads (which flags its quotes as community-added and unverified), BrainyQuote, and pablopicasso.org — prints it bare, with no date, work, or citation.

Because the wording cannot be tied to any dated Picasso work or statement, no copyright status can honestly be assigned. It is not established as a public-domain text, and there is no identifiable rights-holder for a phrase with no located source; the status is simply undetermined.

Fact-check

Why the attribution doesn’t hold

The sentence is pinned to Picasso across the entire quote-web, yet it sits in none of his documented words — and the one source occasionally named for it does not contain it.

✕
The exact wordingPablo Picasso Unsourced

No speech, letter, book, or interview by Picasso is known to contain this sentence. It is absent from Wikiquote’s sourced statements and has never been investigated by Quote Investigator — the classic profile of a feel-good line grafted onto a famous name.

✕
The alleged source“Conversation avec Picasso,” Cahiers d’Art (1935) Doesn’t contain it

When a source is offered at all, it is this 1935 conversation recorded by Christian Zervos. But full reproductions of that text — on Wikiquote and in this Art in Theory transcript — contain no occurrence of ‘imagine’ or ‘imagination’ at all.

✓ What Picasso is actually documented to have said about reality in 1935 is a different thought entirely: “There is no abstract art. You must always start with something. Afterward you can remove all traces of reality” (Conversation with Picasso, 1935).

Context

Why it sticks to Picasso

Picasso is a natural host for a line like this. He was famously quotable, openly hostile to the idea that art merely copies the visible world, and endlessly cited — so a tidy aphorism about imagination ‘becoming real’ slots neatly into his myth. That plausibility is exactly what lets an unsourced line travel: it feels like something he would say, so no one stops to ask where he said it.

The irony is that Picasso’s documented remarks push in a subtler direction. In the 1935 conversation he insists you must “always start with something” real before you can strip away its traces — imagination working on reality, not simply conjuring it. The popular quote flattens that into a motivational slogan he is not recorded as having spoken.

It feels like something Picasso would say — which is precisely why nobody checks whether he did.

Pablo Picasso

1881–1973 · Spanish painter, sculptor & co-founder of Cubism

Pablo Ruiz Picasso (1881–1973) was a Spanish painter and sculptor who, with Georges Braque, invented Cubism and reshaped twentieth-century art through works from Les Demoiselles d’Avignon to Guernica. He gave relatively few formal statements about his art; the best-documented are the 1923 interview with Marius de Zayas (“Picasso Speaks”) and the 1935 “Conversation avec Picasso” recorded by Christian Zervos. His prolific, aphoristic reputation has made him one of the internet’s favourite magnets for feel-good lines he never actually uttered.
