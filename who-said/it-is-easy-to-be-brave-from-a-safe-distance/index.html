Who really said it

It is easy to be brave from a safe distance

Written by Aesop fl. c. 620–564 BC · semi-legendary Greek fabulist
✓ Attribution status: Verified

It is the closing line of Aesop’s fable “The Wolf and the Kid” (Perry Index 98), an ancient tale. This exact English wording is confirmed as the ending of the fable in Joseph Jacobs’s 1894 translation; other translators render the wolf’s reply quite differently.

The fable behind the line

Fable
“The Wolf and the Kid”
Collection
The Fables of Æsop (Perry Index 98)
Translation
Joseph Jacobs, London: Macmillan, 1894 (pp. 40–41)
Attribution
Aesop — traditional; ancient Greek fable tradition

A KID was perched up on the top of a house, and looking down saw a Wolf passing under him. Immediately he began to revile and attack his enemy. “Murderer and thief,” he cried, “what do you here near honest folks’ houses? How dare you make an appearance where your vile deeds are known?” “Curse away, my young friend,” said the Wolf. “It is easy to be brave from a safe distance.”

The famous one-liner is the wolf’s closing retort, functioning as the fable’s moral. In the ancient Greek tradition the same point is made more tersely — the Babrius version has the wolf answer, in effect, that it is the kid’s place, not the kid, doing the talking.

  • The fable is genuinely ancient — catalogued as Perry Index 98, with a surviving Babrius version whose wolf answers “Thy place abuses me.”
  • Wikiquote lists it among Aesop’s sourced quotes, tied to “The Wolf and the Kid.”

Fact-check

Right author, one specific translator’s words

The line is authentically Aesopic — but the memorable English phrasing belongs to one 1894 translator, and quote sites usually present it as a free-floating aphorism with no fable attached.

The crisp modern one-liner is Jacobs’s rendering of the wolf’s reply in his 1894 edition. Other translators word the same moment very differently — and the ancient Babrius version has the wolf say only that the kid’s place, not the kid, is doing the talking.

Context

Why the line lands

The fable is tiny: a kid (young goat) stands safely on a rooftop and hurls insults at a wolf below. The wolf, unable to reach him, simply notes that the bravado costs the kid nothing — it is the height of the roof, not the courage of the goat, doing the shouting.

“Curse away, my young friend.”

The ancient version’s point

In the surviving Greek tradition (the Babrius version), the wolf’s reply lands on location rather than distance: it is not the kid who mocks him, but the kid’s place. Jacobs’s 1894 “It is easy to be brave from a safe distance” is a free, aphoristic modernization of that same idea — which is why it reads so much more like a stand-alone maxim than the older, more literal renderings.

The fabulist

About Aesop

AE

Aesop

fl. c. 620–564 BC · semi-legendary Greek fabulist

Aesop is the name traditionally attached to a large body of ancient Greek fables — short animal tales, each pointing to a moral. Whether a single historical Aesop authored them is doubtful; the stories circulated orally and were gathered and reshaped over centuries by later hands such as Babrius and Phaedrus. “The Wolf and the Kid” is one of these traditional fables, and its punchline about false courage has entered English chiefly through Victorian translators.
