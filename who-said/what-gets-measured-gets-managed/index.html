Who really said it

What gets measured gets managed

Not Peter Drucker — an unsourced management platitude No documented author In print by 1998 · no originator ever established
? Attribution status: Disputed ?Reuse status: Rights unconfirmed

No book, essay, lecture or interview by Drucker has ever been produced that contains this line, and researcher Danny Buerkli — citing the Drucker Institute’s own 2013 essay disowning the sibling maxim “If you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it” — reports that Drucker never said this one either ↗. The phrase was already a stock management saying before anyone thought to check — Gordon Bethune used it as a chapter title in From Worst to First in 1998, and when Simon Caulkin printed the best-known long version in The Observer in 2008 he called it a platitude that should have been throttled at birth, not a line of his own.

How we verified this

  • Start with the specialists: Wikiquote’s Peter Drucker page ↗ has no entry for this line in any section, sourced or otherwise, and a site search of Quote Investigator ↗ returns nothing found — so there is no existing verdict to defer to, and no earliest attestation on record.
  • The strongest denial comes from Drucker’s own institution. In July 2013 the Drucker Institute published “Measurement Myopia” ↗, in which Paul Zak recounts being corrected by the Institute’s Zach First: the maxim he had been quoting was never Drucker’s, and Drucker’s real view was two-sided.
  • For the exact five words the chain is second-hand and inferential, and worth stating plainly: the Institute’s published denial covers the sibling maxim “If you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it,” and the five-word line appears nowhere in that essay. Danny Buerkli links to it and extends the denial to these five words too ↗; his post reports no correspondence with the Institute, so what stands on its own is the absence — no Drucker source containing the line has ever been located. His correction note also reassigns the famous long version from Ridgway to Simon Caulkin.
  • Reading Caulkin’s Observer column ↗ settles a second question: he treats the short form as an existing management platitude and offers the long version as its correction, so he is not the phrase’s author either. Full-text book scans back that up — the line was a chapter title in 1998 ↗.

Fact-check

Four things people get wrong about this line

This is a quote with a name problem at every level: the short form, the long form, the sibling maxim and the ancestor line are each credited to someone who cannot be shown to have written them.

✕
The five-word linePeter Drucker no source

No Drucker book, essay, lecture or interview has been produced containing it. Danny Buerkli, citing the Drucker Institute’s own 2013 essay, reports that Drucker never said it ↗, and Wikiquote’s Peter Drucker page ↗ carries no entry for the line in any of its sections.

✕
The long “full proposition”V. F. Ridgway wrong claimant

The extended version — the one adding that measuring and managing can be pointless and even harmful — is routinely pinned on Ridgway’s 1956 paper. It is in fact Simon Caulkin’s own formulation, printed in The Observer on 10 February 2008 ↗; Buerkli appended a correction to his own article after Scott B. Weingart flagged the error.

✕
The sibling maximPeter Drucker and W. Edwards Deming inverted

“If you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it” is credited to both. The Drucker Institute’s 2013 essay ↗ says flatly that Drucker never said it, and Deming put the sentence in The New Economics only to call it a costly myth ↗.

~
The ancestor lineMason Haire unsourced credit

Peters and Waterman’s In Search of Excellence (1982) is where most readers met “What gets measured gets done”: a long time ago, the organization theorist Mason Haire said it ↗, they write — naming no work, page or date, which is where the trail for that variant also goes cold.

✓ The honest answer is that nobody knows who first said it. It was common enough by 2008 that Caulkin could dismiss it as a management platitude ↗, and the only thing that can be established with confidence is who didn’t say it.

Context

Why the misattribution matters

The line does real work in the world. It is the sentence on the slide before the dashboard, the justification for the target, the reason a metric becomes a mandate — and it borrows all of its authority from a name that was never attached to it. Strip the name away and it is what Simon Caulkin called it in The Observer in 2008: a platitude, true enough to be repeated and vague enough to be dangerous.

The irony runs deeper. The 1956 paper the saying is usually traced back to, V. F. Ridgway’s “Dysfunctional Consequences of Performance Measurements” ↗, was an argument against indiscriminate metrics — interviewers rushing interviews to hit interview counts, investigators cherry-picking easy cases to hit quotas, managers deferring maintenance to set production records. Ridgway’s paper even discussed Drucker’s own 1954 list of measurement areas as an example of the multiple-measures approach he was picking apart. A warning got compressed into a mantra, and a name got stapled on.

A warning about measurement became a slogan for it, and the man credited with the slogan said the opposite.

Read the full story

What Drucker actually wrote sits on both sides of the ledger. In Management: Tasks, Responsibilities, Practices (1973) he tied work to accountability, deadlines and the measurement of results. But in 1990, advising Bob Buford, he described a leader’s first role — relationships, mutual confidence, the creation of a community — as something that cannot be measured or easily defined. The Drucker Institute published both quotations in the same 2013 essay, precisely to correct the slogan.

The saying also has a family. “What gets measured gets done” appears in Peters and Waterman’s In Search of Excellence (1982), where it is credited to “the organization theorist Mason Haire” from “a long time ago” — no page, no work, no date. And “If you can’t measure it, you can’t manage it” is pinned on Drucker and on W. Edwards Deming, though Deming published it only to call the idea a costly myth.

The man who didn’t say it

About Peter Drucker

PD

Peter Drucker

1909–2005 · Austrian-American management writer · Clarke Professor of Social Science and Management, Claremont Graduate University

Born in Vienna on 19 November 1909 and dead in Claremont, California, on 11 November 2005, Peter Drucker did more than anyone to make management a serious discipline, and held the Clarke professorship at Claremont Graduate University from 1971 until his death. His actual position on measurement was two-sided, not slogan-shaped: he wrote in Management: Tasks, Responsibilities, Practices that work implies accountability, a deadline and the measurement of results — and he also told his client Bob Buford in 1990 that a leader’s first role, the building of trust and community, cannot be measured or easily defined. He is the most-credited name on this quote and the one name that can be ruled out.

Provenance

No primary source exists for this wording. The fullest documented form of the phrase appears in Simon Caulkin, “The rule is simple: be careful what you measure,” The Observer, 10 February 2008. The paper the saying is commonly traced to is V. F. Ridgway, “Dysfunctional Consequences of Performance Measurements,” Administrative Science Quarterly 1, no. 2 (September 1956): 240–47.
