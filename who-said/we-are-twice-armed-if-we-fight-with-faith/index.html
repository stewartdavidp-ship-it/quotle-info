Who really said it

We are twice armed if we fight with faith

Not Plato — an unsourced modern paraphrase William Shakespeare (literary root) English playwright & poet, 1564–1616 · the exact “faith” wording is undated & anonymous
? Attribution status: Disputed

The line appears in no dialogue or letter of Plato and is missing from Wikiquote’s curated Plato page. Its memorable “armed by a just cause” idea traces to Shakespeare’s ‘Henry VI, Part 2’ — “Thrice is he armed that hath his quarrel just” — while the specific “twice armed…with faith” phrasing is an anonymous modern rewording later pinned on Plato.

Disputed — unsourced modern paraphrase

What stronger breastplate than a heart untainted? / Thrice is he armed that hath his quarrel just, / And he but naked, though locked up in steel, / Whose conscience with injustice is corrupted. ↑ the likely literary root — Shakespeare, not Plato; note it says “thrice” and “quarrel just,” not “twice” and “faith”

The popular “We are twice armed if we fight with faith” is a loose modern paraphrase of this sentiment. No comparable line exists anywhere in Plato’s dialogues, and the framing of ‘faith’ as a martial virtue is foreign to his vocabulary of ‘aretē’ (excellence) and ‘pistis’ (mere belief/opinion).

How we traced it

  • The line is absent from Wikiquote’s Plato page ↗, which lists Disputed and Misattributed quotes but not this one.
  • Aggregators such as Goodreads ↗ credit Plato but cite no dialogue and flag that the entry is community-added and unverified.
  • The Quotations Page ↗ lists it only under the compilation “Poor Man’s College” — a quote book, not a work of Plato.
  • The closest literary ancestor — our own reading of the parallel, not an externally documented lineage — is Shakespeare’s “Thrice is he armed that hath his quarrel just” ↗.

The exact modern wording has no identified author or date, so its rights status is indeterminate; the underlying Shakespeare passage it echoes (c. 1591) is firmly in the public domain.

Fact-check

The attribution problem

The line is one of thousands of uplifting one-liners assigned to Plato by quote sites without a supporting text. It is not a translation of anything in the Platonic corpus.

✕
Incorrectly attributed to Plato

The quote does not appear in any of Plato’s dialogues or letters, and it is absent from Wikiquote’s Plato page ↗. Sites that credit him — Goodreads ↗, BrainyQuote ↗ — name no work.

✓ There is no evidence Plato wrote or said this; the closest documented source for the idea is Shakespeare’s “Thrice is he armed that hath his quarrel just” ↗ in ‘Henry VI, Part 2.’

Context

Why the sentiment sticks

The appeal is obvious: a compact promise that conviction multiplies strength. That timeless-sounding cadence is exactly what makes it easy to bolt onto a famous ancient name — and Plato, as shorthand for ‘wise Greek philosopher,’ is a magnet for orphaned aphorisms.

But the specific machinery of the line — being ‘armed’ extra times by the righteousness of one’s cause — is a distinctly English, Shakespearean image, later softened from ‘a just quarrel’ into the gentler, more devotional ‘faith.’

Plato talked of excellence and knowledge; the ‘twice-armed by faith’ image is Shakespeare’s, reworded.

The likely literary root

Whose idea it really is

WS

William Shakespeare

1564–1616 · English playwright & poet

William Shakespeare gave the ‘armed by a just cause’ image its enduring form in Henry VI, Part 2 (c. 1591), where King Henry says “Thrice is he armed that hath his quarrel just.” The modern “twice armed…with faith” line is a later, anonymous softening of that thought — not Shakespeare’s exact words, and certainly not Plato’s.
