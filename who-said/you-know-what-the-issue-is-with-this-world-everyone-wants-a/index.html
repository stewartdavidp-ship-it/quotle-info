Wikiquote files this under “Misattributed” on both Carroll’s page and the Wonderland page, and names the real source: it is spoken by Jefferson (Sebastian Stan) in “Hat Trick”, the seventeenth episode of the first season of ABC’s Once Upon a Time, first aired on 25 March 2012 and written by Vladimir Cvetko and David H. “And storybooks are based on what? Imagination. Where does that come from? It has to come from somewhere. You know what the issue is with this world? Everyone wants some magical solution for their problem and everyone refuses to believe in magic.” Once Upon a Time, “Hat Trick” (2012), as transcribed on Wikiquote

Jefferson is arguing with Emma about whether storybooks are less real than history books; the famous sentence is the end of that argument, not a standalone aphorism. Note the wording: “some magical solution for their problem”, with no comma before “and”. Wikiquote’s own misattribution entry quotes the drifted version, which is a fair measure of how far the popular form has travelled.

Wikiquote: Once Upon a Time — "Hat Trick" ↗

How we verified this

  • Carroll’s Wikiquote page ↗ lists this line under “Misattributed” and states it plainly: “Though written in contemporary idiomatic English, this has been recently cited on the Internet on various ‘quotations’ websites… as having being written by Lewis Carroll in Alice in Wonderland… It was actually a line spoken by a character named Jefferson in the TV series Once Upon a Time in a 2012 episode entitled ‘Hat Trick’.” The Wonderland page ↗ carries the same entry.
  • Wikiquote’s transcript of the series ↗ has the scene itself, under “Hat Trick [1.17]” — and that is where the second finding comes from. The transcribed line is “Everyone wants some magical solution for their problem and everyone refuses to believe in magic.” The version in circulation reads “a magical solution to their problem, and…”. Even the debunk quotes the drifted wording.
  • Wikipedia’s article on the episode ↗ supplies the credits we quote: season 1, episode 17, first aired on ABC on 25 March 2012, written by Vladimir Cvetko and David H. Goodman, directed by Ralph Hemecker, with Sebastian Stan as the Mad Hatter / Jefferson.
  • We downloaded the Project Gutenberg texts of ten of Carroll’s English books — 2.41 million characters — Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland ↗ (in two editions), Through the Looking-Glass ↗, The Hunting of the Snark, Phantasmagoria and Other Poems, Three Sunsets and Other Poems, Sylvie and Bruno, Sylvie and Bruno Concluded, A Tangled Tale, The Game of Logic and Symbolic Logic — flattened every line break so no phrase could hide across a line ending, and searched the lot. “magical solution” and “believe in magic” each occur zero times. A site-wide insource: search of Wikiquote for “refuses to believe in magic” returns exactly three pages: Carroll’s and Wonderland’s, both listing it as a misattribution, and Once Upon a Time, where the real line is. Nothing else on the site carries it.

Television writing is work for hire, so the rights sit with the production company rather than the credited writers: Once Upon a Time was produced by ABC Studios and this episode first aired in 2012, well inside the term. Context

Why a 2012 script reads as Victorian

Once Upon a Time built its whole premise on public-domain fairy tales, so its characters carry names readers already know. Put a speech about magic in the mouth of someone called the Mad Hatter and you have manufactured a quotation that feels sourced — the name does the work a citation would normally do, and nobody checks which Hatter.

The wording drift is the more interesting half. A line that travels by being retyped picks up small improvements: “some…for” becomes the smoother “a…to”, and a comma arrives to give the sentence a beat. By the time Wikiquote wrote its correction, the tidied version was the one everybody had, so the correction quotes it too.

Read the full story

In context, Jefferson is not dispensing wisdom. He is holding Emma Swan in his house and trying to force her to make a magic hat work, and the argument is his case that storybooks are no less real than history books. The famous sentence is the end of a coercive speech, which is not how it reads on a poster.

Carroll’s Hatter, for comparison, is not mystical at all. His scene is a quarrel with Time as though Time were a person he had offended at a concert, after which the clock sticks at six and it is always tea-time. That is a joke about language, not a thought about belief.

About the writers

CG

Vladimir Cvetko & David H. Goodman

Credited writers · “Hat Trick”, Once Upon a Time season 1 (ABC Studios, 2012)

The episode carries a shared writing credit: Vladimir Cvetko and David H. Goodman ↗, the latter an American television writer and producer who was on Once Upon a Time as a writer and executive producer across its 2011–2018 run. A shared credit means the sentence cannot honestly be handed to one of them, so we name both, as the episode does. The director was Ralph Hemecker; the speaker on screen is Sebastian Stan.
