Not Nelson Mandela — an evolving proverb (earliest form: Pliny the Elder) No single author — a proverb Mandela never sourced Sentiment dates to Pliny the Elder, c. AD 77; linked to Mandela only from 2001

There is no record of Nelson Mandela ever saying or writing this line: the Nelson Mandela Foundation’s archive returns nothing, and the earliest known Mandela attribution appears in an Australian newspaper in 2001. The underlying idea — that things look impossible only until they are done — traces back nearly 2,000 years to Pliny the Elder and a long chain of later writers ↗.

The paper trail

  • Pliny the Elder, Naturalis Historia (c. AD 77): “How many things, too, are looked upon as quite impossible, until they have been actually effected?” — the earliest form traced by Quote Investigator ↗.
  • Rocket pioneer Robert H. Goddard, Scientific American (1921): “everything is impossible until it is done”; SF author Robert Heinlein, Between Planets (1951): “Everything is theoretically impossible, until it’s done” — per Quote Investigator ↗.
  • Earliest Mandela attribution: the Illawarra Mercury (Wollongong, Australia), 21 April 2001 — a dozen years before Mandela’s death, via Quote Investigator ↗.
  • The Nelson Mandela Foundation has no record of him saying it, its senior researcher Sahm Venter told Africa Check ↗.

The ancient source (Pliny) is firmly public domain. The exact modern wording is an anonymous, unsourced proverb with no identifiable rights-holder — and, importantly, no verified tie to Nelson Mandela.

Fact-check

Who it really wasn’t

This is one of the most widely shared ‘Mandela’ quotes on the internet — printed on posters, mugs and graduation cards — yet it cannot be found in anything Mandela is documented to have said or written.

✕
Incorrectly attributed to Nelson Mandela

The Nelson Mandela Foundation’s searchable archive returns no results for the line, and senior researcher Sahm Venter told Africa Check ↗ the Foundation has no record of him saying it. The earliest Mandela attribution located by Quote Investigator ↗ is a 2001 Australian newspaper item — not a Mandela speech.

✕
Sometimes credited to Robert Heinlein or Robert H. Goddard rare

Both used close cousins of the line — Goddard in Scientific American (1921) and Heinlein in Between Planets (1951) — but neither coined the exact modern wording, and the idea predates them by centuries, per Quote Investigator ↗.

✓ Bottom line: there is no evidence Nelson Mandela ever said it. The line is an anonymous, evolving proverb whose earliest form belongs to Pliny the Elder, c. AD 77 ↗.

Context

Why the misattribution stuck

The line fits the popular image of Mandela — endurance, the long road to freedom, victory that looked impossible until it arrived — so perfectly that it feels like something he must have said. That plausibility is exactly what lets a floating proverb attach itself to a famous name and stay there.

In reality the thought is one humanity has reached for repeatedly: Pliny the Elder framed it as a rhetorical question in the first century, and the twentieth century supplied crisper versions from a rocket scientist and a science-fiction novelist before the polished modern wording drifted onto Mandela’s name around 2001.

It feels like something Mandela must have said — which is exactly how a proverb borrows a famous name.

The documented lineage, step by step

Pliny the Elder, Naturalis Historia (c. AD 77): “How many things, too, are looked upon as quite impossible, until they have been actually effected?” Daniel Wilson echoed it in Prehistoric Man (1862).

Robert H. Goddard wrote in Scientific American (1921): “everything is impossible until it is done.” Robert Heinlein gave it near-modern form in Between Planets (1951): “Everything is theoretically impossible, until it’s done.”

The first attribution to Mandela that researchers have found is a 2001 Illawarra Mercury article in Australia — over a decade before his death in 2013, and with no link to any actual Mandela address or writing.

The real lineage

An idea older than the misattribution

PE

Pliny the Elder (earliest source of the idea)

c. AD 23/24–79 · Roman naturalist & author

Pliny the Elder was a Roman author, naturalist and military commander whose encyclopedic Naturalis Historia (c. AD 77) preserves the earliest known form of this thought: “How many things, too, are looked upon as quite impossible, until they have been actually effected?” The sentiment resurfaced across the centuries — in Daniel Wilson (1862), rocket pioneer Robert H. Goddard (1921) and SF writer Robert Heinlein (1951) — long before its crisp modern wording was pinned, without evidence, on Nelson Mandela around 2001.
