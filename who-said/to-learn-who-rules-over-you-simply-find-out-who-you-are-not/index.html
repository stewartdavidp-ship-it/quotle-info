Who really said it

To learn who rules over you, simply find out who you are not allowed to criticize

Not Voltaire — actually Kevin Alfred Strom (paraphrased) Kevin Alfred Strom b. 1956 · American white nationalist broadcaster
? Attribution status: Disputed ©Reuse status: In copyright

Voltaire never wrote this. The thought — and the sentence it was smoothed out of — belongs to Kevin Alfred Strom, an American neo-Nazi broadcaster, who said it on his American Dissident Voices radio programme on 14 August 1993: “To determine the true rulers of any society, all you must do is ask yourself this question: Who is it that I am not permitted to criticize?” The familiar wording is not even Strom’s own — it is an anonymous internet rewrite, first documented credited to Voltaire in 2007.

This is the actual passage the meme descends from, and the context is not incidental. PolitiFact describes the 1993 broadcast as antisemitic ↗, and Strom’s next line makes the rhetorical question point at a specific group. Recast as a Voltaire epigram, a piece of conspiracist antisemitism is laundered into Enlightenment wisdom about free speech — which is precisely why the misattribution has been so durable.

How it travelled

  • 14 August 1993 — Kevin Alfred Strom broadcasts “All America Must Know the Terror That Is Upon Us” ↗ on American Dissident Voices, containing the rhetorical question the modern line is built from.
  • 2007 — the earliest Voltaire attribution on record. Barry Popik documents it on a hobbyist forum, MiniatureHorseTalk.com ↗, already in the polished “To learn who rules over you…” form. By 2012 it was running as “Quote of the Day” on multiple sites.
  • 2017 — Strom publishes an article titled “Voltaire Didn’t Say It,” claiming the phrase himself; the same year, Nicholas Cronk’s Voltaire: A Very Short Introduction (Oxford, 2017) notes at p. 122 that Strom first used it in 1993 ↗.
  • January 2022 — Rep. Thomas Massie tweets it at Anthony Fauci over Voltaire’s name. PolitiFact rates it False and the Voltaire Foundation confirms the line is absent from Voltaire ↗.

Fact-check

Why Voltaire gets the credit

This is a double error: the wrong author, and words he never wrote. Voltaire is one of the internet’s great quote magnets — an anti-clerical wit with a reputation for aphorisms about free speech, which makes almost any clever line about censorship look plausible over his name. (The most famous “Voltaire” quote of all, “I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it,” was written by his biographer Evelyn Beatrice Hall in 1906.) Here the borrowed authority did real work: it moved a line from a neo-Nazi broadcast into mainstream circulation.

✕
The attributionVoltaire (1694–1778) Misattributed

The phrase appears nowhere in his writings or correspondence. Oxford’s Voltaire Foundation lists it among the one-liners Voltaire never said ↗, and fact-checkers found no trace of it in his collected works or correspondence ↗. Voltaire specialist Paul Gibbard put it plainly: the spirit is “not un-Voltarian” [sic], which is exactly why it sticks — but “there are lots of quotations that are attributed to Voltaire that aren’t actually by him, and that’s one of them.”

✕
The wordingAn anonymous internet rewrite Paraphrase

Strom did not write the sentence as it circulates. His 1993 original is a rhetorical question; the crisp imperative version is someone else’s edit. Strom himself has conceded his maxim “exists online and in print in a dozen different variations, some of them probably more elegant than my original” ↗.

✕
Repeated in good faithRep. Thomas Massie, John Cusack, and countless memes Amplified it

Massie tweeted it over Voltaire’s name at Anthony Fauci in January 2022, drawing a False rating from PolitiFact ↗; actor John Cusack retweeted an antisemitic cartoon carrying the line over Voltaire’s name in June 2019, then apologised (“Made a mistake retweeting that — as i said — and sorry”). There is no reason to think either knew the source — that is the point of a laundered quote.

✓ The honest attribution is: an anonymous paraphrase of Kevin Alfred Strom, American Dissident Voices, 14 August 1993 — which is where Wikiquote files it, under “Misattributed” ↗. Not Voltaire, and not quite Strom’s words either.

Context

Why it matters

Most misattributions are harmless — a nice thought lands on a famous name and nobody is worse off. This one is different, because the misattribution is the mechanism. Strom’s question was not an abstract principle about power; it was posed in an antisemitic broadcast, and the unnamed group it points at was the point. Stripped of that context and stamped with Voltaire’s name, it becomes an Enlightenment maxim about free speech that anyone can repeat in good conscience.

That is what makes it worth tracing rather than merely correcting. Two Voltaire scholars, the Voltaire Foundation, an etymologist and three fact-checking outlets have all independently reached the same conclusion, and the man who said it first has claimed it in public. The line has better documentation than most quotes people do get right — the difficulty was never evidence, it was that the true source is one almost nobody wants to cite.

The false attribution isn’t a footnote to this quote’s history. It’s the reason the quote travelled.

A note on the other “Voltaire” free-speech quote

“I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it” is also not Voltaire’s. It was written by Evelyn Beatrice Hall in The Friends of Voltaire (1906), as her own summary of his attitude rather than a translation of anything he wrote. Voltaire’s reputation as the patron saint of free expression rests substantially on sentences other people wrote for him — which is precisely the reservoir this 1993 line was able to tap.

Kevin Alfred Strom

b. 1956 · American white nationalist broadcaster · founded National Vanguard, 2005

Kevin Alfred Strom is an American white nationalist and neo-Nazi activist, born in 1956 and later based in Virginia. He worked as a broadcast engineer for the National Alliance under William Luther Pierce and hosted American Dissident Voices, a weekly radio hour espousing antisemitic views, going on to found the splinter group National Vanguard in 2005. He pleaded guilty in 2008 to possession of child pornography and was sentenced to 23 months in prison. He is named here purely as a matter of documented provenance — this page traces where the sentence came from, which is a separate question from anything else about the man or his politics.
