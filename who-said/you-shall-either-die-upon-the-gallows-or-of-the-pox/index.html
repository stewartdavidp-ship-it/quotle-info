Who really said it

You Shall Either Die Upon the Gallows or of the Pox

Not Gladstone to Disraeli — Lord Sandwich, to Samuel Foote John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich 1718–1792 · British statesman, First Lord of the Admiralty
? Attribution status: Disputed ?Reuse status: Rights unconfirmed

The insult and its reply are genuine 18th-century repartee, but the people usually named are wrong. The earliest evidence found by Quote Investigator ↗ is in The European Magazine of January 1784, where Lord Sandwich asks the comedian Samuel Foote which will finish him first, “a certain disease, or an intimate acquaintance with the gallows” — and Foote replies that it depends whether he embraces his lordship’s mistress or his principles. The version starring Gladstone and Disraeli was not printed until 1950.

The real exchange

Spoken by
John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich (1718–1792)
Spoken to
Samuel Foote (1720–1777), comedian and theatre manager
Earliest evidence
The European Magazine, January 1784
This wording
Percival Stockdale, Memoirs, 1809, vol. 1, pp. 317–318

“Foote,” (said lord Sandwich) “I have often wondered what catastrophe would bring you to your end; but I think, that you must either die of the p—x, or the halter.”—“My lord,” (replied Foote instantaneously) “that will depend upon one of two contingencies;—whether I embrace your lordship’s mistress, or your lordship’s principles.”

As printed in Percival Stockdale, The Memoirs of the Life and Writings of Percival Stockdale (London, 1809), volume 1, page 318, which we read in the scanned volume ↗. “P—x” is the pox; the “halter” is the hangman’s rope. No documented source uses the modern “you shall either die upon the gallows or of the pox” phrasing.

How we verified this

  • The earliest evidence located by Quote Investigator ↗ is in The European Magazine of January 1784, headed “Bon Mot of the late Sam. Foote”, in which Lord Sandwich asks Foote “which do you think will happen to you first, the experience of a certain disease, or an intimate acquaintance with the gallows?”
  • The wording most people half-remember was printed in 1809 by Percival Stockdale, who set the scene at the beef-steak club in Covent Garden. We read the passage in volume 1, page 318, of the scanned Memoirs ↗.
  • Wikiquote’s Samuel Foote page ↗ carries the same Stockdale text, sourced to the 1809 Memoirs by way of The Yale Book of Quotations.
  • The Gladstone-and-Disraeli casting is modern. Quote Investigator ↗ finds it first in George E. Allen’s Presidents Who Have Known Me (1950), page 237 — 166 years after the Foote version was in print.

The 18th- and 19th-century texts that carry this exchange — The European Magazine (1784) and Stockdale’s Memoirs (1809) — are long in the public domain. The modernised wording quoted on this page, however, matches none of them, so it cannot be tied to a specific published text and no rights status is asserted for it.

Fact-check

Everyone this has been pinned on

The riposte is too good not to reassign, and it has been handed to five different pairs of antagonists over two centuries. Only the first pair is supported by evidence close to the event.

✕
The popular claimWilliam Gladstone and Benjamin Disraeli Not Disraeli

The Victorian rivals were dropped into the roles only in 1950, in George E. Allen’s memoir Presidents Who Have Known Me. Nothing contemporary connects either man to the exchange, per Quote Investigator ↗. Wikiquote lists it among Disraeli’s misattributed quotations ↗.

✓
The actual exchangeLord Sandwich and Samuel Foote, by 1784 Earliest documented

The European Magazine printed it in January 1784, seven years after Foote’s death, as a bon mot of his; Stockdale gave the fuller version in 1809. These are the earliest accounts, and Quote Investigator ↗ concludes Foote is the leading candidate for coining the reply.

✕
Also credited toJohn Wilkes, James Quin, Charles James Fox, Mirabeau Floating anecdote

John Wilkes was substituted for Foote in 1828; the actor James Quin, dead since 1766, was given it in a joke book of about 1816; Charles James Fox appeared in the role in a 1968 Commons debate; and Lord Brougham in 1844 recorded the exchange as Wilkes’s specifically in order to contradict a French version crediting Mirabeau. Quote Investigator ↗ notes it is unlikely Lord Sandwich walked into this twice.

✓ On the earliest evidence the words were Lord Sandwich’s, aimed at Samuel Foote, who supplied the famous reply — not an exchange between Gladstone and Disraeli, per Quote Investigator ↗.

Context

Why it mattered

Sandwich was a genuinely notorious figure — First Lord of the Admiralty, a leading persecutor of the radical John Wilkes, and a man whose private life was public property. An insult about disease and hanging thrown across a convivial table was crude even by the standards of the beef-steak club, which is exactly what makes the reply land.

Foote’s answer works because it accepts the premise and turns the choice back on Sandwich: both fates are available, and which one arrives depends on which part of his lordship’s life you adopt. It is the earliest surviving example of a joke structure that later generations kept re-casting with whoever the current villain was.

It depends whether I embrace your lordship’s mistress, or your lordship’s principles.

Read the full story

Lord Brougham, printing the Wilkes version in 1844, added a personal note that shows how these stories travelled: he said he had heard it from the Duke of Norfolk, who claimed to have been present, many years before the French Revolution — and he printed it specifically to contradict a French account that gave the line to Mirabeau. Even the people passing it on knew it was contested.

The version that names Wilkes was published in 1828 in a book on the clubs of London, which declined to print the setup at all on grounds of decency: “We cannot now detail the whole anecdote; it is, however, so well known, that a slight allusion will recall it.” Per Quote Investigator ↗.

The speaker

About Lord Sandwich

JM

John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich

1718–1792 · British statesman · First Lord of the Admiralty

John Montagu, 4th Earl of Sandwich, was a British politician who served three terms as First Lord of the Admiralty, including through the American War of Independence. He is remembered less for his administration than for two things attached to his name: the sandwich, and a reputation for private licence and political ruthlessness that made him a standing target for the wits of his day. His pursuit of the radical John Wilkes is the reason later tellers found it so easy to move this exchange onto Wilkes.
