Who really said it

The reasonable man adapts himself to the world; the unreasonable one persists in trying to adapt the world to himself

Written by George Bernard Shaw 1856–1950 · Irish playwright, critic & Fabian socialist
✓ Attribution status: Verified

Shaw wrote this as a maxim under the heading “Reason” in “Maxims for Revolutionists,” the aphorism appendix to his 1903 play Man and Superman. The wording is confirmed against the printed text; the widely-quoted form simply drops his concluding line, “Therefore all progress depends on the unreasonable man.”

Context

Shaw’s point about progress

Shaw framed the maxim as a defense of the awkward, uncompromising reformer. Its force lives in the sentence usually left off: because the “unreasonable” person refuses to accommodate the world as it is, “all progress depends on the unreasonable man.”

Therefore all progress depends on the unreasonable man.

Why the wording varies

The circulating version typically replaces Shaw’s colon with a semicolon and stops after “to himself,” turning a three-part argument into a tidy contrast. Quoting the full maxim restores Shaw’s actual thesis about who drives change.

1856–1950 · Irish playwright, critic & Fabian socialist

George Bernard Shaw (1856–1950) was an Irish-born playwright, critic and polemicist, and a leading figure in the Fabian socialist movement. Man and Superman (1903) is among his best-known plays, and the “Maxims for Revolutionists” appended to it are a rich source of his aphorisms. He won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1925.
