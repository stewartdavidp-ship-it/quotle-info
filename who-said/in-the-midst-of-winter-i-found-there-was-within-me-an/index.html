Who really said it

In the midst of winter, I found there was, within me, an invincible summer

Not Camus’s wording — a paraphrase of a real line Albert Camus 1913–1960 · French-Algerian novelist & essayist, Nobel laureate 1957
? Attribution status: Camus wrote the sentence — but not in these words. ©Reuse status: In copyright

Camus genuinely wrote this thought — it is the closing sentence of the central section of “Retour à Tipasa,” collected in L’Été (Gallimard, 1954): “Au milieu de l’hiver, j’apprenais enfin qu’il y avait en moi un été invincible.” A section break follows, and Camus leaves again for “Europe and its struggles.” But this particular English sentence matches neither standard published translation. It is the winter verse of an anonymous inspirational litany that circulates as a Camus letter.

How we know

  • The line is genuine Camus, and the specialists say so: Wikiquote lists it in its sourced section — not among the misattributed — crediting “Return to Tipasa” (1954) and giving the Lyrical and Critical Essays (1968), p. 169 rendering “In the depths of winter, I finally learned that within me there lay an invincible summer” — Wikiquote, Albert Camus ↗. (Quote Investigator has no article on this quote.)
  • The work checks out: “Retour à Tipasa” is one of eight texts collected in L’Été, published by Gallimard in 1954, per the Camus scholarly society — Société des Études camusiennes ↗. The essay revisits the Roman ruins Camus had celebrated in Noces (1939).
  • But the English does not check out. Justin O’Brien’s 1955 translation — the volume that first carried “Return to Tipasa” into English — reads “In the middle of winter I at last discovered that there was in me an invincible summer.” Searched end to end, the full text contains no instance of “midst of winter” or “within me, an invincible”: The Myth of Sisyphus and Other Essays, trans. Justin O’Brien (1955) ↗.
  • The popular wording’s real home is a fabricated letter: “My dear, In the midst of hate, I found there was, within me, an invincible love … In the midst of chaos … an invincible calm … In the midst of winter, I found there was, within me, an invincible summer … Truly yours, Albert Camus.” Our sentence appears in it verbatim, with no source given ↗.

Fact-check

Where the popular wording actually comes from

This is the unusual case where the idea is genuinely Camus and the sentence is not. The English everyone quotes has no home in any standard translation — its real parent is a devotional rewrite that has been circulating, unsourced, for decades.

~
The wordingAn anonymous inspirational ‘poem’ paraphrase

The line is one verse of a litany: “In the midst of hate, I found there was, within me, an invincible love. In the midst of tears … an invincible smile. In the midst of chaos … an invincible calm” — then the winter/summer verse, signed off “Truly yours, Albert Camus.” That repeated “In the midst of X, I found there was, within me, an invincible Y” frame is what gives the famous sentence its odd, comma-heavy shape: it was built to match the other verses, not to translate the French. Goodreads carries the whole thing with no source attached ↗. It circulates in French too, retitled “J’ai trouvé en moi” and billed as a Camus poème — though Camus wrote essays here, not poems.

✕
The happy endingNot Camus fabricated

The most-shared version continues: “And that makes me happy. For it says that no matter how hard the world pushes against me, within me, there’s something stronger — something better, pushing right back.” None of that is in the essay. Searched against O’Brien’s full text, “pushes against,” “makes me happy,” “pushing right back” and “no matter how hard” return zero hits. Camus’s actual next move is to leave Tipasa for “Europe and its struggles.” Goodreads hosts the coda version, again with no source ↗.

✕
“It’s the O’Brien translation”Quote sites and content farms false claim

A common line online is that “In the midst of winter, I found there was, within me” is Justin O’Brien’s — supposedly the most faithful rendering. It is not. O’Brien’s text ↗ reads “In the middle of winter I at last discovered that there was in me an invincible summer,” and the phrase “midst of winter” appears nowhere in the volume. The same pages tend to give a mangled French original as well — a useful tell.

✓ Camus wrote the sentence — in French, at the close of the central section of “Retour à Tipasa” in L’Été (Gallimard, 1954). What he did not write is the version on the poster: the standard English, as Wikiquote’s sourced section documents ↗, runs “In the depths of winter, I finally learned that within me there lay an invincible summer” — which Wikiquote locates at Lyrical and Critical Essays (1968), p. 169, the Knopf volume Ellen Conroy Kennedy translated ↗ — or “In the middle of winter I at last discovered that there was in me an invincible summer,” which is Justin O’Brien’s 1955 text ↗. Quote the thought freely — just don’t quote the poem.

Context

Why it mattered

Camus wrote it about going home. Tipasa is a Roman ruin on the Algerian coast, and the young Camus had celebrated it in Noces (1939) as a place inhabited by gods — sun, sea, wormwood, stone. He went back in the early 1950s, after the Occupation and the war, half-expecting the visit to prove only how much had been lost. It rained. The place seemed closed to him.

Then the light returned, and with it the recognition that the ruins of Tipasa were, as he put it, younger than the bomb damage of modern Europe. The famous sentence is the last line of that recognition — not a greeting-card affirmation but a hard-won note at the end of an essay about carrying beauty back into a fight. The line that follows it in the book is Camus leaving again for “Europe and its struggles.”

Camus wrote it in French, at the end of an essay about going home. The English everyone quotes was written by someone else.

The two real English translations

Justin O’Brien (1955), in The Myth of Sisyphus and Other Essays: “In the middle of winter I at last discovered that there was in me an invincible summer.”

Ellen Conroy Kennedy (1968), in Lyrical and Critical Essays, p. 169: “In the depths of winter, I finally learned that within me there lay an invincible summer.”

Both are defensible readings of “Au milieu de l’hiver, j’apprenais enfin” — O’Brien takes “au milieu” literally as “middle,” Kennedy reaches for the more idiomatic “depths.” Neither produces “I found there was, within me.” That phrasing exists to make the sentence rhyme with the other verses of a poem Camus never wrote.

The author

AC

Albert Camus

1913–1960 · French-Algerian novelist, essayist & playwright; Nobel Prize in Literature, 1957

Born in French Algeria to a nearly illiterate mother and a father killed in the First World War, Albert Camus grew up poor in Belcourt, Algiers, and never lost the Mediterranean light that saturates his essays. He edited the Resistance paper Combat under the Occupation and won the Nobel Prize in 1957, three years before dying in a car crash at forty-six. He is best known for L’Étranger and La Peste, but the lyrical essays — Noces, L’Été — are where the sun, the sea and the “invincible summer” actually live.
