Who really said it

If food is so scarce, why hasn't Gandhi died yet?

Wavell's paraphrase of Churchill, July 1944 Archibald Wavell 1883–1950 · Viceroy of India, 1943–1947 — the words are his, describing Churchill
? Attribution status: Disputed ©Reuse status: In copyright

This is not a Churchill quotation, but it is not invented either. The words come from the private journal of Field Marshal Lord Wavell, Viceroy of India, in July 1944: ‘Winston sent me a peevish telegram to ask why Gandhi hadn’t died yet! He has never answered my telegram about food.’ That is Wavell’s summary of a telegram, not a transcript of it. Churchill’s actual message asked why Gandhi, released two months earlier on medical grounds, was already active in politics. It says nothing about food. The famine clause in the popular version — ‘if food is so scarce’ — appears in no source we could find. The likeliest route is paraphrase: the diary line is quoted in later books, and a 2021 New Yorker interview restated it with a famine framing that no document carries.

How we traced it

  • Wavell’s journal was published in 1973, edited by Penderel Moon. The entry is his own compressed, exasperated account of a telegram he had received, written for himself. The exclamation mark is doing a lot of work: he is characterising Churchill’s tone, not quoting his sentences.
  • The telegram Wavell was reacting to survives, and asks a different question. Gandhi had been released from detention in May 1944 on grounds of ill health; by July he was again politically active. Churchill’s message asked how that squared with the medical reports on which the release had been agreed. It is a suspicious and unpleasant enquiry about whether he had been deceived. It is not a wish that Gandhi would starve.
  • The second sentence — that Churchill had never answered his telegram about food — is Wavell’s separate complaint about grain shipments for India. The popular quotation reads the two as one and produces a sentence nobody wrote: if food is so scarce, why hasn’t Gandhi died yet? That welding is the likeliest explanation for how the famine framing got in, but it is our reconstruction, not something the sources document.
  • The timing matters and cuts both ways. July 1944 is after the worst of the Bengal famine, so the remark is not a response to the mass deaths of late 1943 — but Wavell’s reproach about the unanswered food telegram shows he was still pressing on supply. Langworth notes those food requests went mainly to Leo Amery, and that grain had been arriving since January 1944 the documents compared ↗.

Fact-check

What the documents say

There is a real document behind this, which is why it is worth handling carefully rather than dismissing. Three things have gone wrong between the journal and the meme.

✕
The wordsWavell's, not Churchill's paraphrase

‘Why Gandhi hadn’t died yet’ is Wavell writing in his own journal about the tone of a telegram. It is indirect speech with an exclamation mark. Quoting it as Churchill’s sentence, in quotation marks, misrepresents what kind of record it is.

✕
The food clauseAdded later not in the source

‘If food is so scarce’ appears in no source we could find. The likeliest route is paraphrase — commentators quoting the diary line supplied a famine framing, joining it to Wavell’s separate second sentence, his complaint that his own telegram about food had gone unanswered. That join is our reading, not a documented one.

✕
The dateJuly 1944, not 1943 wrong occasion

The quotation is almost always presented as a response to the 1943 Bengal famine at its height. The journal entry is from July 1944, and its subject is Gandhi’s recovery after his release from detention in May of that year on medical grounds.

✕
Churchill's actual telegramAbout the medical reports documented

He asked how Gandhi’s remarkable recovery and immediate return to politics squared with the medical reports on which the British had agreed to release him. Suspicious, ungenerous, and about detention policy — not about starvation.

✕
What is not in disputeThe famine record separate question

Correcting the quotation settles nothing about the famine. Bengal’s dead are estimated at anywhere from under one million to nearly four million, with around two million the most commonly cited figure; the War Cabinet’s decisions on grain shipping and Indian exports are documented and are argued about on the documents, not on this sentence.

✓ Wavell wrote it, in his own journal, in July 1944, about a telegram concerning Gandhi’s health. Churchill’s surviving message asks about medical reports and does not mention food both texts ↗.

Context

The famine, factually

Between 1943 and 1944 famine killed a number commonly put at around two million people in Bengal — estimates range from under one million to nearly four million — then part of British India. The immediate causes were a cyclone and crop disease in late 1942, the loss of Burmese rice imports after the Japanese advance, wartime inflation and hoarding, a scorched-earth denial policy that removed boats and rice stocks from coastal districts, and provincial export controls that trapped grain elsewhere in India. Relief was not declared for months.

The contested question is what London did with the requests that followed. Wavell and Amery pressed repeatedly for grain shipments; the War Cabinet approved far less than was asked for, citing shipping demands elsewhere. Historians disagree about how much of the shortfall was logistics and how much was priority. That argument is conducted on cabinet minutes and telegrams — and it does not need this quotation, which is why the quotation is worth getting right.

Two sentences in a private journal. Fuse them and you get a quotation nobody wrote, on a date it could not have been said.

The sequence, in order

Late 1942 — cyclone, crop disease, and the loss of Burmese rice imports after the Japanese occupation of Burma.

1943 — famine in Bengal; deaths estimated at between under one million and nearly four million across 1943–44, most commonly put at around two million.

October 1943 — Wavell arrives as Viceroy and uses the army to move grain and distribute relief.

May 1944 — Gandhi, detained since 1942, is released on grounds of ill health.

July 1944 — Churchill telegraphs Wavell querying Gandhi’s recovery against the medical reports; Wavell records his irritation in his journal, and notes his own food telegram is still unanswered.

1973 — the journal is published, and the entry begins its second life as a Churchill quotation.

Popularly credited author

Why it gets pinned on Churchill

WC

Winston Churchill

1874–1965 · British Prime Minister, 1940–1945 and 1951–1955

Sir Winston Churchill held hostile views of India and of Gandhi and expressed them freely; his Secretary of State for India, Leo Amery, recorded remarks in his diaries that are worse than anything on this page. So the misquotation lands on ground that has already been prepared. The point of correcting it is not to defend him. It is that a fabricated sentence is easy for his defenders to knock down, and doing so lets the documented record — the export decisions, the refused shipping, the unanswered telegrams — slip out of the argument along with it.
