Happiness lies in the joy of achievement and the thrill of creative effort

Spoken by Franklin D. Roosevelt 1882–1945 · 32nd U.S. president · spoke it in 1933
✓ Attribution status: Verified

From FDR’s First Inaugural Address, delivered at the U.S. Capitol on 4 March 1933 at the depth of the Great Depression. His actual words were “Happiness lies not in the mere possession of money; it lies in the joy of achievement, in the thrill of creative effort” — confirmed in Yale’s Avalon Project ↗ and the American Presidency Project ↗.

Context

Why it mattered

Roosevelt delivered these words on 4 March 1933, with roughly a quarter of the American workforce unemployed and the banking system in collapse. The passage was not a standalone aphorism about self-fulfillment but part of a pointed argument: that the country’s recovery required valuing work and achievement over “mere monetary profit.”

In its full form — “Happiness lies not in the mere possession of money; it lies in the joy of achievement, in the thrill of creative effort” — the line drives toward his next sentence about “the mad chase of evanescent profits.” The popular short version, stripped of the money clause, reads as a generic motivational slogan and loses that Depression-era edge.

He wasn’t praising ambition in the abstract — he was indicting the “mad chase of evanescent profits.”

How the wording drifted

Quote databases such as BrainyQuote, PassItOn, and Goodreads circulate the condensed form, which is why it now appears on posters and social media without the opening clause. The attribution to FDR is nonetheless correct — this is a case of loose quotation, not false authorship.

The exact historical text, preserved in multiple public-domain archives, keeps the contrast Roosevelt intended. Where precision matters, cite the full sentence.

About Franklin D. Roosevelt

FDR

Franklin D. Roosevelt

1882–1945 · 32nd President of the United States

Franklin Delano Roosevelt (1882–1945) was the 32nd President of the United States, elected to four terms and serving from 1933 until his death in 1945. He took office at the depth of the Great Depression, and his First Inaugural Address — best remembered for “the only thing we have to fear is fear itself” — framed the New Deal’s moral case against the unchecked pursuit of profit. The line about happiness comes from that same speech.
