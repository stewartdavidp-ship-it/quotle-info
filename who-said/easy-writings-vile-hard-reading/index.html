Who really said it

Easy Writing's Vile Hard Reading

Not Ernest Hemingway — actually Richard Brinsley Sheridan Richard Brinsley Sheridan 1751–1816 · Anglo-Irish playwright, poet and Whig MP
? Attribution status: Disputed ✓Reuse status: Free to reuse

The line is the second half of a couplet in Sheridan’s satirical poem “Clio’s Protest; or, The Picture Varnished” — composed in 1771 and printed anonymously in 1772: “You write with ease, to shew your breeding; / But easy writing’s vile hard reading.” Quote Investigator ↗ finds Hemingway using only a reworded version and concludes he spoke the adage after it was already in circulation — the couplet predates his birth by more than a century.

How we know

  • Quote Investigator ↗ traces the couplet to Sheridan’s “Clio’s Protest,” composed 1771 and distributed in 1772 in an anonymous London collection, and lists no earlier source for the wording.
  • Thomas Moore’s 1825 life of Sheridan quotes the couplet as “You write with ease, to show your breeding, / But easy writing’s curst hard reading” — readable in the Project Gutenberg text ↗, where Moore notes how many people quote the line without knowing its source.
  • Wikiquote ↗ lists both wordings under Sheridan, citing Clio’s Protest and, for the “vile” form, the Concise Oxford Dictionary of Quotations via Moore’s Life of Sheridan.
  • The Hemingway line is a different sentence. Wikiquote ↗ records only “Easy writing makes hard reading,” sourced to Samuel Putnam’s Paris Was Our Mistress (1947), p. 128.
✓Public domain

The couplet appeared in print in 1772 and the poem was published under Sheridan’s name in 1819; Thomas Moore’s Memoirs, which carries the widely reproduced “curst” version, dates from 1825. All are long past copyright and in the public domain in the United States. Note that Samuel Putnam’s 1947 memoir — the source for Hemingway’s different wording — is a separate, still-protected work; only the Sheridan couplet is free to reuse.

Fact-check

Everyone it has been pinned on

This couplet has been reassigned roughly once a century. Sheridan published it anonymously, which left it up for grabs almost immediately.

~
The modern creditErnest Hemingway Repeated it, didn't write it

Samuel Putnam’s 1947 memoir Paris Was Our Mistress records Hemingway saying “Easy writing makes hard reading” around 1926 — a different wording, and Putnam explicitly files it under “copybook triteness” among the apothegms Hemingway dropped in conversation. Quote Investigator ↗ concludes there is substantive evidence Hemingway spoke the adage only after it was already in circulation. Wikiquote ↗ carries the Hemingway line in exactly that shorter form, never as “vile hard reading.”

✕
The nineteenth-century creditLord Byron A misread stanza

An 1868 newspaper columnist hesitantly credited Byron, and an 1875 magazine article stated it outright. The likely cause is the “art of easy writing” stanza of Beppo (1818) — numbered XLVIII in the 1818 first edition and LI in the later Coleridge/Prothero text: “Oh that I had the art of easy writing / What should be easy reading!” — which you can read at Wikisource ↗. Byron uses “easy writing” and “easy reading.” He never writes “hard reading” at all.

✕
The mirror-image maximNathaniel Hawthorne Different saying, also misattributed

“Easy reading is damn hard writing” is a separate proverb often confused with this one and routinely credited to Hawthorne, largely because Maya Angelou attributed it to him in interviews. Quote Investigator ↗ found no substantive evidence Hawthorne used it and provisionally credits Thomas Hood, who wrote “the easiest reading is d__d hard writing” in 1837 — in quotation marks, suggesting it was already current.

✓ The words are Sheridan’s, from a poem he published without his name in 1772 and that only carried his name from an 1819 edition onward — documented in full at Quote Investigator ↗.

Context

A hatchet job that outlived its target

“Clio’s Protest” is not a writing manual. It is a young Sheridan’s anonymous demolition of a rival’s verse, and the couplet is the punchline of a jab: the poet in question writes fluently to advertise his gentility, and the reader pays for it. The insult is that fluency was the point — ease as social display rather than craft.

Two and a half centuries later the line survives detached from its victim, its poem and its author, as a piece of workshop advice. That detachment is exactly what made it available for reattribution: a floating aphorism with no visible parentage attaches itself to whichever famous writer is nearest, and for the twentieth century that was Hemingway.

Sheridan meant it as an insult about a specific bad poet. It survives as general advice to everyone.

How the wording drifted: vile, curst, cursed, very

The adjective was never stable. The 1772 printing has “vile.” An 1821 New Monthly Magazine item censored it to “d— hard reading.” Thomas Moore’s 1825 Memoirs of Sheridan prints “curst.” An 1835 Philadelphia paper softened it to “very.” An 1826 North American Review notice dropped the adjective entirely: “Easy writing is hard reading.” All of these are documented at Quote Investigator ↗.

Moore, writing in 1825, already treated the couplet as ownerless — “one of those stray fragments, whose parentage is doubtful” — before restoring it to Sheridan. Its authorship was being lost within fifty years of publication, which is why every later century has had its own candidate.

The real author

Who actually wrote it

RS

Richard Brinsley Sheridan

1751–1816 · Anglo-Irish playwright, poet and Whig MP

Born in Dublin in 1751, Richard Brinsley Sheridan wrote The Rivals and The School for Scandal before he was thirty, bought a controlling share of the Theatre Royal, Drury Lane, and then spent three decades in the House of Commons as one of its most feared orators. “Clio’s Protest” is early, minor, anonymous work — a young man’s hatchet job on a rival versifier — and it is the least famous thing he wrote that everybody quotes. He died in 1816; the poem did not carry his name in print until an 1819 edition, three years after his death.
