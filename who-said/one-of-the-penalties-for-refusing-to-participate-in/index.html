Who really said it

One of the penalties for refusing to participate in politics is that you end up being governed by your inferiors

Attributed to Plato c. 428–348 BCE · Athenian philosopher, founder of the Academy
≈ Attribution status: Attributed

The thought is authentically Plato’s — it comes straight from Republic Book 1, 347c–d, where Socrates says the chief penalty for good men who refuse to rule is to be governed by someone worse. But this exact English wording is a modern paraphrase; no scholarly translation renders it “refusing to participate in politics … governed by your inferiors.”

Fact-check

Right author, loosened words

This one is not credited to the wrong person — the idea really is Plato’s. The catch is the wording: the viral sentence is a modern paraphrase presented as a verbatim quotation, and it has spawned further variants that also get pinned on Plato.

✕
Circulated as a verbatim Plato translation: “… refusing to participate in politics … governed by your inferiors” paraphrase

No scholarly edition renders it this way. Shorey’s actual translation of 347c is “the chief penalty is to be governed by someone worse if a man will not himself hold office,” per Perseus ↗.

A later simplification — WIST ↗ lists it, and the related “good men pay for indifference” form, among the downstream paraphrases of 347c rather than Plato’s own words.

Context

Why it matters

In Republic Book 1, Socrates is arguing that the truly good are reluctant to rule — they take office not for money or honor but under a kind of compulsion. The compulsion he names is fear: if capable people hold back, government falls to those less fit, and the good must then live under them.

That is why the line lands as an argument for civic participation. Whether in Shorey’s “governed by someone worse” or the modern “governed by your inferiors,” the point is the same — opting out is not neutral; it hands power to people you would not choose.

The chief penalty is to be governed by someone worse if a man will not himself hold office.

On Emerson and the later variants

Ralph Waldo Emerson paraphrased the passage in his essay “Eloquence” in Society and Solitude (1870): “Plato says that the punishment which the wise suffer who refuse to take part in the government, is, to live under the government of worse men.” That Victorian rendering is a likely ancestor of today’s snappier “governed by your inferiors.”

The blunter offshoots — “the price of apathy is to be ruled by evil men” and “the penalty good men pay for indifference to public affairs is to be ruled by evil men” (used on the mid-century Constitution Party letterhead) — drift further from the Greek while keeping Plato’s name attached.

About Plato

P

Plato

c. 428–348 BCE · Athenian philosopher

Plato was an Athenian philosopher, a student of Socrates and teacher of Aristotle, and founder of the Academy. His Republic is a dialogue on justice and the ideal state; the line about being ruled by worse men appears in Book 1, where Socrates argues that the best people take office reluctantly, chiefly to avoid being governed by their inferiors.
