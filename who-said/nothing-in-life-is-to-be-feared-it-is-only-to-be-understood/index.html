Who really said it

Nothing in life is to be feared, it is only to be understood

Attributed to Marie Curie 1867–1934 · Polish-French physicist and chemist
≈ Attribution status: Attributed

Widely credited to Marie Curie and fully in character — the first sentence appears in French in a 1951 medical-journal citation and was quoted as hers by chemist Glenn T. Seaborg in 1968 — but no primary source in Curie’s own hand has been located, and the familiar second sentence (“…so that we may fear less”) appears to be Seaborg’s, not hers.

The quote circulates almost everywhere as two sentences, but the halves have different origins. Only the first is credited to Curie in early sources; the second surfaces as a closing line in a 1968 article by chemist Glenn Seaborg.

How we traced it

  • The first sentence appears in French — «On ne doit rien craindre dans la vie—il suffit de comprendre» — in a 1951 Laval médical citation catalogued by todayinsci.com ↗, whose editor notes the primary source is uncertain.
  • Chemist Glenn T. Seaborg quoted only the first sentence as Curie’s in ‘Need We Fear Our Nuclear Future?’ (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, 1968) ↗; the second sentence sits outside his quotation marks.
  • Quote databases such as Goodreads ↗ circulate the full two-sentence version as Curie’s.
  • Notably, the line does not appear among the sourced entries on Marie Curie’s Wikiquote page ↗.

Provenance and rights are murky: the sentence credited to Curie is first documented in print in 1951 (she died in 1934), and the popular second sentence originates in Glenn Seaborg’s 1968 article, which is not in the public domain. Treat the familiar two-sentence version as a modern composite.

Fact-check

The catch

The problem here isn’t the author — it’s the words. The famous version welds a sentence Marie Curie almost certainly never wrote onto one that is credibly hers.

✕
Words wrongly credited to Marie Curie (the second sentence) the real issue

The line ‘Now is the time to understand more, so that we may fear less’ appears outside quotation marks in Glenn T. Seaborg’s 1968 article ↗ and reads as his own closing remark; only the first sentence is quoted as Curie’s. The editor of todayinsci.com ↗ concludes the second sentence ‘is solely the words of Glenn T. Seaborg.’

✓ Curie is credibly the source of the first sentence; the memorable ‘…so that we may fear less’ coda was added by chemist Glenn Seaborg around 1968 ↗, and no primary Curie source has been found for either half.

Context

Why it resonates

Marie Curie spent her life handling materials whose danger she understood better than almost anyone alive — and worked with them anyway. The sentiment that fear yields to understanding fits a scientist who carried tubes of radium in her pockets and kept a faintly glowing sample by her bedside.

So the attribution rings true even without a paper trail: it sounds like her because it matches how she lived.

Nothing in life is to be feared, it is only to be understood.

Read the full story

The line took its now-famous two-sentence shape in a Cold-War setting. Glenn Seaborg, chairman of the U.S. Atomic Energy Commission, invoked Curie at her 1967 centenary in Warsaw and, in the published version of that talk, followed her sentence with his own coda — ‘Now is the time to understand more, so that we may fear less’ — a plea for calm about the nuclear future. The two halves have travelled together as ‘Curie’ ever since.

About Marie Curie

MC

Marie Curie

1867–1934 · physicist and chemist, two-time Nobel laureate

Marie Curie (born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw) was a Polish-French physicist and chemist who pioneered research on radioactivity — a term she coined. She won two Nobel Prizes, in Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911), and remains the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences. She died in 1934 of aplastic anemia widely linked to her long exposure to radiation.
