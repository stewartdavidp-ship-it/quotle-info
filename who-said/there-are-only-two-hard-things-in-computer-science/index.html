Who really said it

There are only two hard things in Computer Science: cache invalidation and naming things

Phil Karlton — attested, never documented Phil Karlton 1947–1997 · engineer at Xerox PARC, DEC, Silicon Graphics and Netscape
≈ Attribution status: Attributed ?Reuse status: Rights unconfirmed

The credit is almost certainly right and it rests entirely on people’s word. Phil Karlton was a Netscape engineer who died in a car accident in Italy on 20 June 1997. His son, David Karlton, wrote in 2017 that his father ‘touted’ the phrase during his Netscape years. Colleagues remember hearing it from him. But there is no email, no memo, no slide, no mailing-list post and no recording — and the earliest instances anyone has located online are from December 2005, eight years after his death. This one cannot be closed, and it is more interesting for that.

How we traced it

  • David Karlton is the anchor. Writing on 4 December 2017, he opens: ‘Years ago, when my dad Phil Karlton was working at Netscape, he touted a now-infamous phrase.’ Note what he does and does not claim — that his father repeated it, not that his father is provably the first person ever to say it his post ↗.
  • The earliest instances located online are from December 2005 — a post by Tim Bray, and one by Kellan Elliott-McCrea dated 23 December 2005 — both already crediting Karlton by name, and neither citing anything but memory. Bray has said he first heard the line around 1996 or 1997 the 2005 post ↗.
  • Martin Fowler, who collects this sort of thing carefully, wrote it up as a favourite saying for which he could not find a satisfactory URL — a neat description of the whole problem Fowler’s note ↗.
  • The dates leave a gap nobody has filled. Karlton was at Netscape from the mid-1990s and died in June 1997; the first written trace appears in 2005. Everything in between is oral transmission among people who worked with him.
  • One further claim we could not check: a commenter on David Karlton’s post says he heard Phil say it at Carnegie Mellon around 1970, which would push it back a quarter of a century. It is a single unverified recollection, and we record it as that rather than as a finding.

Fact-check

How good is the evidence?

Nobody seriously proposes a rival author, and the industry has credited Karlton consistently for twenty years. The honest problem is narrower: nothing written by him has ever been produced, and the first written trace postdates his death by eight years.

David Karlton’s 2017 post states plainly that his father used the phrase at Netscape. A family member with no incentive to invent it is good evidence — though he is attesting to a memory, not producing a document the post ↗.

Fabricated attributions usually appear late, after a saying is already famous. This one carries Karlton’s name in the earliest instances anyone can find, from people who worked in his orbit. That is the opposite pattern.

Context

Why it resonates

It is funny because it is a list of two things that sound trivial and are not. Caching is the standard way to make software fast, and knowing when a cached copy has gone stale is a problem with no general solution. Naming looks like typing and is really the act of deciding what a thing is.

There is also a joke inside the joke that programmers rarely say out loud: naming things is hard because the second problem is the first one in disguise. A name is a cache of your understanding, and it goes stale the moment the code changes underneath it.

A name is a cache of your understanding. It goes stale the moment the code moves.

The timeline, and the hole in it

1947: Philip Lewis Karlton born in Los Angeles, 28 February.

Career at Xerox PARC, Digital and Silicon Graphics; principal work on the X11 sample server.

Mid-1990s: at Netscape, working on SSL, listing himself as Principal Curmudgeon. This is when colleagues remember hearing the line.

20 June 1997: Phil and Jan Karlton are killed in a car accident in Italy. He was fifty.

December 2005: the earliest written instances anyone has located appear online, already crediting him.

4 December 2017: his son David publishes the attestation that is now the standard citation.

The gap from the mid-1990s to 2005 has never been filled by a document, and at this distance it probably never will be.

About Phil Karlton

PK

Phil Karlton

1947–1997 · engineer at Xerox PARC, DEC, Silicon Graphics and Netscape

Phil Karlton (1947–1997) spent his career on infrastructure other people built on: he was a principal contributor to the X11 sample server that commercial X implementations were based on, and at Netscape he worked on SSL — the encryption that became HTTPS. His own home page there listed his title as ‘Principal Curmudgeon’, which tells you roughly everything about the register the quotation is in. He and his wife Jan were killed in a car accident in Italy on 20 June 1997; he was fifty. He is now known to millions of programmers who could not name a single thing he built.
