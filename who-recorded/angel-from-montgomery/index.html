Who recorded it first

Angel from Montgomery

Not a Bonnie Raitt original — John Prine cut it first, in 1971

Because Bonnie Raitt has sung it in a woman’s voice for fifty years, most listeners file “Angel from Montgomery” as a Bonnie Raitt song. It is a John Prine record. Prine wrote it and recorded it for his self-titled debut album, issued by Atlantic Records in 1971 and cut at American Sound Studios in Memphis with Arif Mardin producing. It sat at side two, track three of an album that reached only No. 154 on the US pop chart — Raitt’s 1974 version on Streetlights is the one that carried the song to a wide audience.

It was cut at American Sound Studios in Memphis, produced by Arif Mardin, and runs 3:44 — a gospel-leaning arrangement led by the studio’s house piano and organ.

Charted Did not chart. Prine’s recording was never a hit single, and the album carrying it managed only No. 154 on the US Billboard pop albums chart in 1972. Half a century later the same album re-entered the Billboard 200 at No. 55, after Prine’s death.

How we know

Atlantic put it out as ninth track on the debut LP — the MusicBrainz entry for the 1971 US pressing, SD 8296 ↗, lists it in that position at 224,426 ms.

Wikipedia’s page on the debut album ↗ supplies the rest of the documentation: Atlantic, Arif Mardin, American Sound Studios in Memphis, and a chart peak of No. 154 in 1972.

The earliest tape is earlier still, and it is still Prine — a November 1970 Chicago club set that stayed in a box until Oh Boy Records issued The Singing Mailman Delivers ↗, where the song is disc two, track seven.

Raitt’s version arrived on Streetlights ↗ in September 1974, produced by Jerry Ragovoy for Warner Bros. and credited to Prine as writer.

Fact-check

Setting the record straight

Raitt’s reading is so completely inhabited that it is routinely mistaken for the source. The recording history is not ambiguous.

The beliefCover, 1974

Bonnie Raitt

Raitt recorded it for Streetlights (Warner Bros., September 1974), three years after the song was already on record. Hers is the version that first attained wide recognition — which is a different achievement from being first.

The originalAtlantic, 1971

John Prine

The first released recording is Prine’s own, side two, track three of his debut album John Prine (Atlantic SD 8296, 1971), produced by Arif Mardin at American Sound Studios in Memphis.

The writerSole credit

John Prine

Prine wrote both words and music. Wikidata, MusicBrainz and Atlantic’s own label credit all name him alone; the composition carries ISWC T-924.850.414-5. Unusually for a song of this kind, the writer and the first recording artist are the same person.

The earliest tapeNovember 1970, unreleased until 2011

John Prine, live in Chicago

A club set from Prine’s residency at the Fifth Peg predates the Atlantic session and includes the song. It stayed unissued until Oh Boy Records released The Singing Mailman Delivers on 25 October 2011. It moves the earliest known recording earlier — but not to a different artist.

John Prine wrote it and recorded it first, in 1971. Bonnie Raitt made it famous in 1974. Both of those things are true, and only one of them is being remembered.

Context

Why the cover swallowed the original

The usual explanation for a cover eclipsing an original is that the cover was a bigger hit. That is not quite what happened here. Raitt’s Streetlights reached No. 80 on the Billboard 200 and Prine’s debut had stalled at No. 154 — respectable and unrespectable respectively, but neither is the kind of chart event that rewrites a song’s authorship in the public mind. What Raitt had instead was fit. Prine wrote the song after being told to write “another song about old people,” and built it around a woman who feels older than she is. Sung by a man, it is an act of imagination. Sung by a woman, it stops sounding like a character study and starts sounding like testimony.

That is the whole mechanism. Raitt did not out-sell the original; she out-belonged it. Then she did the thing that actually decides these questions — she kept singing it, for five decades, on stages and at tributes and eventually at the 2020 Grammy ceremony where Prine received his Lifetime Achievement Award. A song heard live a thousand times in one voice becomes that voice’s property, whatever the label copy says. Prine, who wrote it and recorded it first, spent much of that time standing next to her while she sang it.

The chronology, step by step
  • November 1970 — Prine plays the song during his residency at the Fifth Peg in Chicago. The tape exists but goes unreleased for forty-one years, surfacing only in 2011 on Oh Boy Records’ The Singing Mailman Delivers. It is the earliest known recording of the song, and it is also John Prine.
  • 1971 — Atlantic Records issues John Prine (SD 8296), recorded at American Sound Studios in Memphis with Arif Mardin producing. “Angel from Montgomery” is side two, track three, running 3:44.
  • 1972 — The covers start early and start local. Chicago folk singer Bonnie Koloc records it for Hold On to Me. Carly Simon cuts a version during her No Secrets sessions; it is shelved and does not appear until the 1995 box set Clouds in My Coffee.
  • 1973 — John Denver puts it on Farewell Andromeda, pluralising the title to “Angels from Montgomery.”
  • 1974 — Bonnie Raitt records it for Streetlights (Warner Bros., September 1974), produced by Jerry Ragovoy at the Hit Factory in New York. Hers is the version that first attains wide recognition for the song.
  • 2020 — Raitt performs it at the Grammy ceremony honouring Prine with a Lifetime Achievement Award. Prine dies that April.
She didn’t out-sell the original. She out-belonged it.
JP

The original recording artist — and the writer

John Prine

1946–2020 · American singer-songwriter

Prine was delivering mail in suburban Chicago and playing folk clubs at night when Roger Ebert wandered into the Fifth Peg and reviewed him. Kris Kristofferson, dragged along by Steve Goodman, brought him to New York to open at the Bitter End, where Jerry Wexler heard him and signed him to Atlantic the next day. The debut album that followed in 1971 carried “Angel from Montgomery” alongside “Sam Stone” and “Hello in There.” He won four Grammys across his career and a Lifetime Achievement Award in 2020, the year he died of COVID-19 complications in Nashville.

BR

Popularly credited as the original

Bonnie Raitt

b. 1949 · American singer and slide guitarist

Raitt was four albums into a Warner Bros. career built largely on inhabiting other people’s songs when she cut “Angel from Montgomery” for Streetlights in 1974. She has said the idea that a young man could write his way into the world of a middle-aged woman in a thankless marriage struck her hard as a young feminist, and that the song has probably meant more to her audience than anything else she has recorded. She kept it in the set for decades, often duetting on it with Prine himself. The record is superb and the reading is hers; only the chronology gets misremembered.
