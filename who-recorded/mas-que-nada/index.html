Who recorded it first

Mas que Nada

Not a Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ’66 original — the record came out of Rio in 1963, and possibly 1962

Outside Brazil the song is filed under Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ’66, whose 1966 A&M single carried it onto the American charts. But Mendes did not write it and did not record it first: Jorge Ben ↗ wrote it and cut it himself in Brazil, on his Philips debut Samba Esquema Novo, in 1963. What is genuinely unsettled is what came before that: Wikipedia states the song was first recorded in 1962 by the organist Zé Maria, on the album Tudo Azul — Bossa Nova e Balanço, with Ben himself singing lead. A detailed Brazilian discography counters that the Zé Maria session was indeed taped first but not issued until Continental pressed it in September 1963, after Ben’s own single was already a hit. Either way the trail leads to Jorge Ben — the question is which Jorge Ben vocal reached the public first.

Released Jorge Ben’s own recording, titled “Mas, que Nada!”, opened his debut album Samba Esquema Novo on Philips Records in Brazil, and was also issued as a single. Wikipedia dates the album to 10 January 1963; a detailed Brazilian discography instead places a Continental single around May–June 1963 (catalogue 78-273, with “Por Causa de Você, Menina” as the A-side), a Philips single in July 1963 (P-61.208-H) and the Philips album in late September 1963 (P 632.161 L). We could not reconcile the two accounts.

Charted No chart position is documented for the Brazilian release — the numbers attached to this song all belong to the covers. Brazilian sources simply describe it as the hit that launched Ben’s career at home.

The paper trail

Wikipedia’s article on Samba Esquema Novo ↗ dates Jorge Ben’s Philips debut to 10 January 1963, produced by Armando Pittigliani, and states that it “includes the original recording of the international hit ‘Mas que Nada.’” The track is listed first, at 3:02.

The song’s own Wikipedia article ↗ complicates that: it says the song was “originally recorded in 1962 by” the bandleader Zé Maria, on Tudo Azul — Bossa E Balanço, with Jorge Ben singing lead. No citation is attached to that sentence, which is why this page is filed as disputed rather than settled.

A Brazilian discography post ↗ gives the fullest account and reaches a different conclusion: the Zé Maria album carried the first recordings but was “released by Continental only in September 1963 after the songs became famous” (PPL 12079), by which time Ben’s own single had already appeared on Continental and been reissued by Philips.

The MusicBrainz search API returns a Jorge Ben recording of “Mas, que nada!” at 182,333 ms (3:02) attached to the 1963 release Samba esquema novo — matching the album track length and giving a stable identifier for the original as distinct from Ben’s later re-recordings and the Mendes versions.

Fact-check

What people get wrong

The song is so tied to one 1966 American single that its Brazilian origin has effectively vanished outside Brazil. Here is what the sources actually support.

The beliefCover, 1966

Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ’66

Their A&M single reached number 47 on the Billboard Hot 100 and number four on Easy Listening, and Wikipedia notes it is “better known than Jorge Ben’s original and, to many, the definitive version.” It is a cover.

The writerComposer

Jorge Ben

Every source consulted credits the composition to Jorge Ben. Mendes has never been credited as a writer of it.

The original recordingDisputed, 1962–63

Jorge Ben — or Zé Maria, with Ben on lead vocals

Ben’s own recording opens Samba Esquema Novo (Philips, 1963). Wikipedia, without citation, says the song was first recorded in 1962 by Zé Maria on Tudo Azul — Bossa Nova e Balanço with Ben singing lead; a Brazilian discography says that album was recorded first but not released until Continental issued it in September 1963. We could not resolve which reached the public first.

Not the originRe-recording

The 2006 Black Eyed Peas version

Mendes re-cut the song with the Black Eyed Peas in 2006; it topped charts in Hungary and the Netherlands and reached number six in the UK. It is a re-recording of a cover, two steps from the source.

Jorge Ben wrote “Mas que Nada” and recorded it in Brazil in 1963. Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ’66 covered it in 1966. Whether an earlier 1962 Zé Maria recording — also sung by Ben — beat him to it is genuinely unsettled.

Context

How a Rio single became an American standard

The correction that matters is easy: Jorge Ben wrote the song and recorded it in Brazil before Sérgio Mendes ever touched it. The harder question is why almost nobody outside Brazil knows that. Part of the answer is distribution. Ben’s record was a Brazilian release on Philips, sung entirely in Portuguese, aimed at a domestic audience that made it a hit at home. Mendes’ version was made in Los Angeles, produced by Herb Alpert, and pushed by A&M into the American singles market — a machine Ben’s original was never plugged into.

The other part is that the Mendes record kept re-arriving. A 1998 Nike commercial built around the 1966 recording put it back in front of a generation that had never heard it, and in 2006 Mendes re-cut it with the Black Eyed Peas, topping charts in Hungary and the Netherlands and reaching number six in the UK. Each revival attached the song more firmly to his name. Meanwhile the earliest chapter — the Zé Maria session, where Ben sang lead on someone else’s album before he had a record of his own — is barely known even in Brazil.

The disputed chronology, step by step
  • Jorge Ben writes “Mas, que Nada!” and is performing it around Rio in the early 1960s.
  • The organist and bandleader Zé Maria records it for his album Tudo Azul — Bossa Nova e Balanço, with Ben singing lead. Wikipedia dates this recording to 1962; a detailed Brazilian discography dates the album’s Continental release to September 1963 (catalogue PPL 12079) and says it was held back until the song was already famous. This is the point sources disagree on.
  • Jorge Ben records the song under his own name. Wikipedia gives 10 January 1963 on Philips, as the opening track of Samba Esquema Novo; the same Brazilian discography instead places a Continental single around May–June 1963, a Philips single in July 1963, and the Philips album in late September 1963.
  • Other Brazilian acts cover it almost immediately: Tamba Trio in 1963, Luis Carlos Vinhas in 1964.
  • 1966: Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ’66 release their version on A&M. It reaches number 47 on the US Billboard Hot 100, number four on Easy Listening and number 54 in Canada, and becomes the version most of the world knows.
  • 1998: a Nike commercial using the 1966 Mendes recording restores it to global circulation. 2006: Mendes re-records it with the Black Eyed Peas, reaching number six in the UK and number one in Hungary and the Netherlands.
Ben made the record. Mendes made it travel.
JB

The original recording artist and the songwriter

Jorge Ben

b. 22 March 1939, Rio de Janeiro — Brazilian singer-songwriter and guitarist

Born Jorge Duílio Lima Menezes in Rio de Janeiro, he arrived at the start of the 1960s with a guitar style that fitted neither traditional samba nor the cool geometry of bossa nova, and Billboard would later call him “the father of samba rock.” “Mas, que Nada!” was the song that launched him: his own recording opened Samba Esquema Novo, his 1963 Philips debut. He went on through Jovem Guarda and Tropicália, adopted the name Jorge Ben Jor in 1989, and received the Latin Grammy Lifetime Achievement Award in 2005. Because he both wrote and first sang the song, there is no third party to credit here.

SM

Popularly credited as the original

Sérgio Mendes & Brasil ’66

Sérgio Mendes, 11 February 1941 – 5 September 2024 — Brazilian pianist and bandleader

Mendes, a pianist from Niterói, assembled Brasil ’66 in Los Angeles and signed to A&M, where Herb Alpert produced the group. Their reading of “Mas que Nada” was the group’s first single and their calling card: it reached number 47 on the US Billboard Hot 100 and number four on the Easy Listening chart, and it carried the album Herb Alpert Presents Sergio Mendes & Brasil ’66 to platinum. The arrangement is superb and the record deserves its standing — the only error is chronological. Mendes returned to the song in 2006 with the Black Eyed Peas, reaching number six on the UK Singles Chart, which pushed the association even harder.
