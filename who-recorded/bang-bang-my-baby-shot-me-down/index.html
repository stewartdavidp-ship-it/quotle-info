Who recorded it first

Bang Bang (My Baby Shot Me Down)

Not a Nancy Sinatra original — a 1966 Cher single on Imperial

Because Quentin Tarantino hung Nancy Sinatra’s reading over the opening credits of Kill Bill: Volume 1, a lot of people met the song as hers. It was Cher’s first: she cut it at Gold Star Studios in Los Angeles on 11 February 1966, and Imperial Records issued it as a single on 25 February 1966. It was written by Sonny Bono, then her husband. Far from being an obscure original, Cher’s record went to No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 and became her first million-selling single — the eclipse happened decades later, in a cinema.

What people get wrong

The Nancy Sinatra recording is the one most people have heard, and it is easy to assume the famous version is the first one. It is not.

How we traced the writing

The song is credited to Sonny Bono, who wrote it for Cher; her version was recorded first in 1966.

It has had several famous performances since — Nancy Sinatra’s, and later Nancy Sinatra’s reading in Kill Bill — and the writer is easily lost behind them. It was Bono.

The same page’s other story is the recording (above): Cher cut it first, and later versions carried it further.

Context

How a No. 2 hit lost its own song

The usual shape of these stories is that a forgotten record got outsold by a famous one. This one is stranger: Cher’s original was not obscure at all. It reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100, No. 3 in the UK, and sold over a million copies. What overtook it was not a bigger hit but a better placement — Nancy Sinatra’s album track, sitting unremarked for decades, was picked by Quentin Tarantino for the opening credits of Kill Bill: Volume 1 in 2003.

That is the whole mechanism. Cher’s version was a radio hit for a season; Sinatra’s became a film’s signature, replayed for a generation that had no memory of the chart. Billy Strange’s spare tremolo guitar arrangement suits a revenge picture in a way that Sonny Bono’s bright 1966 production never could, so the cover reads as definitive to anyone who arrived through the cinema.

The chronology, step by step
  • 11 February 1966 — Cher records the song at Gold Star Studios, Los Angeles. Sonny Bono, her husband, wrote it.
  • 25 February 1966 — Imperial Records releases it as a single. It reaches No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 and No. 3 in the UK, and becomes Cher’s first million-selling single.
  • 1966 — It appears on Cher’s album The Sonny Side of Chér.
  • May 1966 — Nancy Sinatra’s version appears on her Reprise album How Does That Grab You?, arranged by Billy Strange. It was not a Hot 100 hit.
  • 1966 — Foreign-language versions top charts elsewhere: Sheila’s French recording reaches No. 1 in France in July, Dalida’s Italian version No. 1 in Italy.
  • 2003 — Quentin Tarantino uses Sinatra’s recording over the opening credits of Kill Bill: Volume 1, and it becomes the version most listeners know.
  • 2005 — UK duo Audio Bullys sample Sinatra’s recording on their electro house track “Shot You Down”; it reaches No. 3 in the UK and No. 20 in Australia.
The original was a million-seller. The cover was a movie.
C

The original recording artist

Cher

b. 1946 · American singer and actress · Imperial Records, 1966

Cherilyn Sarkisian was already half of Sonny & Chér when she recorded this on her own in February 1966, at the age of nineteen. The single was her breakthrough as a solo artist — No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100, No. 3 in the UK, and the first record of hers to sell a million. It appeared on her second solo album, The Sonny Side of Chér ↗.

NS

Popularly credited as the original

Nancy Sinatra

b. 1940 · American singer · Reprise Records, 1966

Sinatra recorded the song the same year for her Reprise album How Does That Grab You? ↗, arranged by Billy Strange, whose tremolo guitar figure is the version’s signature. It is a genuinely great record and the reason the song has a second life: Quentin Tarantino placed it over the opening credits of Kill Bill: Volume 1 in 2003. Sinatra herself credited the revival to “the lonely sound” of Strange’s guitar. The only error is chronological — hers is the cover.

SB

The songwriter

Sonny Bono

1935–1998 · American songwriter, producer and later politician

Bono wrote the song and produced Cher’s recording of it. He and Cher were married at the time, and the Sonny & Cher partnership — his songs, her voice — produced most of her mid-1960s hits. He later left music for politics, serving as mayor of Palm Springs and then in the US House of Representatives.
