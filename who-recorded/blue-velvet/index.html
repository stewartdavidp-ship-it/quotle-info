Who recorded it first

Blue Velvet

Not a Bobby Vinton original — Tony Bennett recorded it on 17 July 1951

Bobby Vinton’s Epic single reached No. 1 in September 1963, and David Lynch’s 1986 film fixed the song in the ear as his, but the record was a cover. Bernie Wayne and Lee Morris wrote it, and Tony Bennett was the first to record it, on 17 July 1951 at Columbia’s 30th Street Studio with the Percy Faith orchestra. Arthur Prysock recorded a rival Decca version on 23 July 1951 that reached shops ahead of Bennett’s — Decca was advertising Decca 27722 in Billboard on 11 August 1951 and the paper reviewed it on 25 August, while Bennett’s Columbia 39555 was not issued until 21 September. So “the original” splits in two depending on the test applied, and this page applies first-recorded; on first-released the answer is Prysock.

Released Two rival singles inside one season. Bennett’s reading — cut 17 July 1951 at Columbia’s 30th Street Studio with Percy Faith conducting, matrix CO 45194 — was issued on 21 September 1951 as Columbia 39555 (and 4-39555 on 45), with “Solitaire” on the flip. Arthur Prysock’s version, recorded 23 July 1951 at Decca’s Pythian Temple studio with an orchestra directed by Sy Oliver, went out as Decca 27722 backed with “The Morningside of the Mountain”, and Decca was already advertising it in Billboard on 11 August 1951.

Charted Bennett’s Columbia single was a hit but never a chart-topper: No. 16 on Billboard’s ‘Records Most Played by Disc Jockeys’ and No. 18 on both ‘Best Selling Pop Singles’ and ‘Most Played Juke Box Records’. Prysock’s Decca side is not recorded as having charted nationally. Cash Box pooled Bennett’s version with the Prysock, Bill Farrell and Norman Kaye records and ranked them as one line, peaking at No. 12 on the chart dated 1 December 1951.

How we know

Decca got there first in the trade press: the label was advertising “Blue Velvet” b/w “The Morningside of the Mountain” as Decca 27722 in Billboard of 11 August 1951 ↗.

Billboard’s Advance Record Releases for 18 August 1951 ↗ lists Decca 27722 under both titles. A week later the paper reviewed the record itself: Billboard of 25 August 1951 ↗ carries the notice for Decca 27722, Prysock with the Sy Oliver orchestra. No weekly issue between 30 June and 28 July 1951 lists the title, and no source names a version recorded before Bennett’s 17 July session — which is the basis for the first-recorded call, since the trade paper’s release listings were never exhaustive.

Bennett’s Columbia single turns up later: Billboard’s ‘Billboard Picks’ of 6 October 1951 ↗ tips “Blue Velvet” by Tony Bennett on Columbia 39555 and points readers to Bill Farrell’s MGM 11062 as well.

The single itself is catalogued: MusicBrainz lists Columbia 39555 ↗, dated 21 September 1951, “Blue Velvet” backed with “Solitaire”.

Fact-check

Three claims, three different answers

Almost everything people believe about this song is true of a different record from the one they mean. Untangling it takes the dates rather than the memory.

The beliefCover

Bobby Vinton

His Epic recording, made 27 May 1963 and released 2 August 1963, topped the Billboard Hot 100 for three weeks from 21 September, and David Lynch’s 1986 film welded it permanently to the title. It is a wonderful record. It is also a cover.

The first recordingOriginal

Tony Bennett

Cut on 17 July 1951 at Columbia’s 30th Street Studio with the Percy Faith orchestra, matrix CO 45194, and issued on 21 September 1951 as Columbia 39555. On the first-recorded test — the one this page applies — Bennett is the original.

The first releaseDisputed

Arthur Prysock

Prysock recorded his Decca version on 23 July 1951, after Bennett, but Decca got it out first: the label advertised Decca 27722 in Billboard on 11 August 1951, the paper listed it in Advance Record Releases on 18 August and reviewed it on 25 August — all before Bennett’s Columbia single appeared on 21 September. Wikipedia’s prose ↗ agrees that Prysock’s “evidently was the first version released, in August 1951”, though the same article’s infobox dates the Decca single to September. The trade press supports the prose, not the infobox.

The writersSongwriters

Bernie Wayne and Lee Morris

Neither Vinton, Bennett nor Prysock wrote it. The Decca, Columbia and Epic issues all carry the Wayne–Morris credit, as do the Clovers’, the Statues’ and Lana Del Rey’s later versions.

Bennett got to the microphone first, Prysock got to the shops first, and Vinton got the No. 1 that everybody remembers.

Context

Why the 1963 record swallowed the 1951 ones

In 1951 the song was the property and the record was merely one performance of it. Publishers pushed sheet music, labels raced rival versions into shops within weeks of each other, and Cash Box pooled four separate “Blue Velvet” singles — Bennett’s, Prysock’s, Bill Farrell’s and Norman Kaye’s — into a single chart line that peaked at No. 12. Nobody was hunting for the definitive recording, because the idea barely existed yet.

By 1963 it did. Vinton cut the song almost as an afterthought: filler for Blue on Blue, an album of titles containing the word ‘blue’, reportedly suggested by the publisher Al Gallico, who sent his secretary out with a dollar for the sheet music. Vinton finished it in two takes and thought little of it. Then it went to No. 1, and then David Lynch built a film around it in 1986 — one in which Isabella Rossellini’s character performs the song on screen — and after that there was only one version most listeners could hear.

The full chronology
  • 1950 — Bernie Wayne and Lee Morris write the song. (Wikipedia dates the composition to 1950 but places Wayne’s Richmond, Virginia inspiration on a 1951 visit, and does not reconcile the two.)
  • 17 July 1951 — Tony Bennett records it at Columbia’s 30th Street Studio, New York, with Percy Faith conducting. Matrix CO 45194.
  • 23 July 1951 — Arthur Prysock records it for Decca at the Pythian Temple, New York, with an orchestra directed by Sy Oliver.
  • 11 August 1951 — Decca advertises Prysock’s Decca 27722 in Billboard. The paper lists it in Advance Record Releases on 18 August and reviews it on 25 August.
  • 21 September 1951 — Columbia issues Bennett’s version as 39555, backed with “Solitaire”. Bill Farrell (MGM 11062) and Norman Kaye also put out 1951 versions.
  • 16 December 1954 — the Clovers record it for Atlantic; the 1955 single reaches No. 14 on Billboard’s R&B Best Sellers in Stores.
  • 1960 — the Statues, a Nashville trio on Liberty, take it to No. 84 — the first version of the song to reach the Hot 100.
  • 27 May 1963 — Bobby Vinton records it in Nashville with Burt Bacharach’s orchestra. Epic releases it on 2 August and it is No. 1 by 21 September.
  • 1986 — David Lynch names a film after the song and features Vinton’s recording in it.
In 1951 nobody was looking for the definitive version. There wasn’t supposed to be one.
TB

The first artist to record it

Tony Bennett

1926–2023 · American traditional-pop and jazz singer

Anthony Dominick Benedetto was the hottest new male singer in America in 1951, riding No. 1 records with “Because of You” and “Cold, Cold Heart”, when Bernie Wayne pitched the song to Columbia’s Mitch Miller and Miller handed it straight to him. Bennett recorded it on 17 July that year with Percy Faith conducting, and it became his last hit of a remarkable twelve months. He kept coming back to it across his career — live at Carnegie Hall in 1962, in duet with k.d. lang in 2011 — even after his pianist Ralph Sharon warned him off sweet ballads of exactly this kind.

BV

Popularly credited as the original

Bobby Vinton

b. 1935 · American pop singer, ‘The Polish Prince’

Stanley Robert Vinton was already having an enormous 1963 — “Blue on Blue” had gone to No. 3 — when the album built around it needed another ‘blue’ title. He cut “Blue Velvet” in two takes in Nashville, expected little of it, and rated his remake of “Am I Blue?” the better bet. It became the record of his life: three weeks at No. 1, No. 5 on the year’s Billboard tally, and a second life at No. 2 in Britain in 1990. The performance is entirely his; only the chronology gets misremembered.

BW

The songwriters

Bernie Wayne & Lee Morris

Wayne 1919–1993 · Morris 1912–1978 · American songwriters

Bernie Wayne wrote more than a thousand songs and a shelf of jingles, among them “There She Is”, long the Miss America theme, and the Chock full o’Nuts tune. He began “Blue Velvet” after a stay at the Jefferson Hotel in Richmond, Virginia, where a guest dressed in blue velvet kept catching his eye; Lee Morris shared the credit. Wayne later recalled being told at the premiere of Lynch’s film that Vinton’s record had been the director’s favourite song as a high-school student in 1963.
