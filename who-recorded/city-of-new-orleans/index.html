Who recorded it first

City of New Orleans

Not an Arlo Guthrie original — Steve Goodman recorded it first, live in Chicago in 1970, on Dunwich

Arlo Guthrie’s 1972 single is the version almost everyone carries in their head, and the Guthrie surname makes the song feel like an inherited piece of American folk tradition. It is not. “City of New Orleans” was written in 1970 by Steve Goodman, a young Chicago songwriter, and first recorded by Goodman himself that same year — not in a studio, but live at the Earl of Old Town folk club in Chicago, for the various-artists LP Gathering at the Earl of Old Town (Dunwich Productions 670), released in 1971. His studio version followed on his self-titled debut album for Buddah Records — catalogue BDS 5096, issued in November 1971. That album was a critical success and a commercial failure, and neither Goodman record produced a hit. Guthrie heard the song from Goodman in person at a Chicago club and cut his own version for Hobo’s Lullaby ↗, which became the only Top 40 hit of his career.

How the record was traced

Goodman’s earliest recording is not the famous one. The Dunwich Records article ↗ states that the 1971 Dunwich Productions LP Gathering at the Earl of Old Town, a live collection of Chicago folk artists, “notably includes the first recording” of Goodman performing the song. The Dunwich album discography ↗ lists it as Dunwich Productions 670, Various Artists, 1971, produced by Jim Post, and the release itself ↗ shows the track at side B6, 3:55.

Praguefrank’s country discography dates that session to 1970, at the Earl of Old Town in Chicago, Jim Post producing, issued on Dunwich 670; SecondHandSongs gives the recording date as 23 March 1970. Either way it sits roughly eighteen months ahead of the Nashville session.

The album entry ↗ dates the Buddah debut to November 1971 and lists the song as a 3:52 album track, produced by Norbert Putnam and Kris Kristofferson — Goodman’s first studio recording of it, and the first version of any kind to reach a national audience.

The MusicBrainz recording ↗ independently pins that studio take to Steve Goodman with a first-release date of 1971 and a runtime of 3:52, attached to the US release on Buddah Records, catalogue BDS 5096.

The song article ↗ gives January 1972, which is sometimes read as a rival date for the album. It is not: it is the date of Goodman’s own Buddah single of the song, BDA 270, backed with “Would You Like to Learn to Dance?” — the B-side the same infobox names, and the single Praguefrank lists. The LP is the November 1971 release, per Billboard’s new-releases listing of November 27, 1971. Neither charted.

No recording of the song by any other artist predates Goodman’s, and Goodman’s own earliest recording is the Dunwich live take, which predates his Buddah studio version. Guthrie’s biography ↗ describes his 1972 version as a cover of Goodman’s song.

Fact-check

Setting the record straight

The song is routinely filed as an Arlo Guthrie original, helped along by his father’s name and by a lyric that sounds like folk tradition rather than a recent copyright. The chronology is not in doubt.

The beliefPopular credit

Arlo Guthrie

His June 1972 Reprise single — No. 18 on the Hot 100, No. 4 Easy Listening — is the version in general circulation, and the only Top 40 hit of his career. It is a cover.

The originalFirst recording

Steve Goodman, 1970

Recorded live at the Earl of Old Town in Chicago in 1970 and issued in 1971 on the Dunwich Productions compilation Gathering at the Earl of Old Town (Dunwich 670). Goodman cut it again in 1971 at Quadrafonic Studios in Nashville for his self-titled Buddah debut (BDS 5096); that album drew good reviews and sold poorly, so neither of his versions reached the audience the cover did.

The writerSole composer

Steve Goodman

Goodman wrote it in 1970 on the Illinois Central line. He is both the songwriter and the first recording artist — there is no third party in this story. Willie Nelson’s 1984 Columbia version topped the country chart and won Goodman the 1985 Grammy for Best Country Song, awarded after his death.

Written by Steve Goodman in 1970 and first recorded by Goodman that same year, live in Chicago. Arlo Guthrie made it famous in 1972.

Context

Why the cover swallowed the original

The interesting question is not why Guthrie’s version was a hit — it is a fine record on a major label — but why Goodman’s got no traction at all. Goodman was an unknown debut artist on Buddah, a label whose reputation was built on bubblegum pop rather than folk, and his album landed as a critical success and a commercial failure. Guthrie arrived at the same song with Reprise behind him, a national audience already built by “Alice’s Restaurant,” and the most loaded surname in American folk music.

That surname is the whole mechanism. A song about a fading passenger train, sung by Woody Guthrie’s son, reads as something handed down rather than something written — as if it had always existed and Guthrie were merely its custodian. Goodman had written it in 1970, on a train, in his twenties. The song’s later life reinforced the illusion: Willie Nelson took it to No. 1 on the country chart in 1984, and by then it had been absorbed into the standards repertoire, where authorship tends to dissolve.

The chronology, step by step
  • 1970 — Goodman writes the song while travelling the Illinois Central line to visit his wife’s family.
  • 1970 — he records it live at the Earl of Old Town, a Chicago folk club, for a various-artists LP produced by Jim Post.
  • 1971 — that recording is issued on Gathering at the Earl of Old Town, Dunwich Productions 670. It is his first recording of the song, and almost nobody hears it.
  • 1971 — he re-records it at Quadrafonic Studios in Nashville, produced by Norbert Putnam and Kris Kristofferson.
  • November 1971 — that studio take is issued on his debut album Steve Goodman, Buddah Records BDS 5096.
  • January 1972 — Buddah releases it as a single, BDA 270, backed with “Would You Like to Learn to Dance?”. It does not chart.
  • Chicago — at the bar of the Quiet Knight, Goodman asks Arlo Guthrie to hear a song; Guthrie agrees if Goodman buys him a beer, then asks to record it.
  • April 1972 — Guthrie’s version appears on Hobo’s Lullaby (Reprise); the single follows in June 1972 and reaches No. 18 on the Hot 100 and No. 4 on Easy Listening.
  • 1973 — the Guthrie single spends two weeks at No. 1 in New Zealand.
  • July 30, 1984 — Willie Nelson releases his version on Columbia; it tops the Hot Country Songs chart.
  • 1985 — Goodman, who died in September 1984, is awarded the Grammy for Best Country Song posthumously.
It sounds inherited. It was written in 1970, by a man in his twenties, on a train.
SG

The original recording artist — and the songwriter

Steve Goodman

1948–1984 · American folk singer-songwriter · Chicago

Steve Goodman (July 25, 1948 – September 20, 1984) was a Chicago folk singer-songwriter who wrote “City of New Orleans” in 1970 after a trip on the Illinois Central line to visit his wife’s family, and first recorded it that year on stage at the Earl of Old Town in Chicago, for the various-artists Dunwich LP Gathering at the Earl of Old Town. The studio version came in 1971 at Quadrafonic Studios in Nashville, for his self-titled Buddah debut ↗, produced by Norbert Putnam and Kris Kristofferson, who had helped him land the deal. The album was praised and did not sell. Goodman lived with leukemia for most of his adult life and died in Seattle at 36; a Grammy for Best Country Song reached him posthumously in 1985, for a song he had already written before most listeners had heard his name.

AG

Popularly credited as the original

Arlo Guthrie

b. 1947 · American folk singer · son of Woody Guthrie

Arlo Guthrie, born July 10, 1947 in Brooklyn, was already famous for the sprawling “Alice’s Restaurant Massacree” when Goodman approached him at the bar of the Quiet Knight in Chicago and asked to play him something. Guthrie agreed on the condition that Goodman buy him a beer. He recorded the song for Hobo’s Lullaby ↗ on Reprise, and the single reached No. 18 on the Hot 100 and No. 4 on Easy Listening in 1972 — the only Top 40 hit he ever had. His reading is unhurried and genuinely lovely, and he has never claimed to have written it; the error is the listener’s, and it is purely chronological.
