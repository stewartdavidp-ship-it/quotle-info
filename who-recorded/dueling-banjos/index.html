Who recorded it first

Dueling Banjos

Not a Weissberg & Mandell original — a 1955 MGM single by Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith with Don Reno, titled “Feudin’ Banjos”

Almost everyone meets this tune through Eric Weissberg and Steve Mandell, whose arrangement for Deliverance became a No. 2 Billboard Hot 100 single and a Grammy winner. It is a cover. Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith composed the piece in 1954 and recorded it first in 1955 for MGM as “Feudin’ Banjos”, playing four-string banjo against Don Reno’s five-string. What makes this misattribution unusual is that the film industry made the same mistake the public did: the December 1972 Warner Bros. single was credited to Weissberg alone, Smith was given no composer credit, and he had to go to law to have his name attached to his own tune.

How we traced it

Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith composed the piece in 1954 and recorded it in 1955 on four-string plectrum banjo, accompanied by the bluegrass five-string player Don Reno Wikipedia ↗.

The physical record survives and can be checked: the Internet Archive’s Great 78 Project transfer of the disc carries label MGM, catalogue number 12006, matrix number 55 S 641, performers Arthur Smith and Don Reno, writer Smith, and, on the other side of the disc, “’Bye ’Bye Black Smoke Choo Choo” Internet Archive ↗.

We looked for anything earlier and found none: the standard originals-and-covers reference names Smith and Reno on MGM in 1955 as the original, listing every other version from 1956 onward, and notes only a loose melodic resemblance to a seventeenth-century Praetorius dance tune The Originals ↗.

Eric Weissberg and Steve Mandell’s arrangement for Deliverance reached No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100 and won Best Country Instrumental Performance at the 16th Annual Grammy Awards in 1974, but the single Warner Bros. issued in December 1972 was credited to Weissberg alone, and the filmmakers did not acknowledge Smith as the composer; he sued and came away with songwriting credit and royalties Wikipedia ↗.

The litigation itself is documented in the legal press: Smith turned down an early Warner Bros. offer of $15,000, then spent two years and about $125,000 on the case before it settled — and said afterwards that the royalty cheques still arrived every 90 days ABA Journal ↗.

Fact-check

Setting the record straight

This is the rare case where the misattribution was not just popular but printed — on the film’s credits and on the record label — and had to be undone by lawyers.

The beliefPopular credit

Eric Weissberg and Steve Mandell, 1972

Their Deliverance arrangement is the version everyone has heard: a No. 2 Billboard Hot 100 single, No. 1 Easy Listening, No. 5 Country, No. 17 in the UK, certified Gold, and the winner of Best Country Instrumental Performance at the 16th Annual Grammy Awards in 1974.

The originalFirst recording

Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith with Don Reno, 1955

Issued on MGM as K12006 under the title “Feudin’ Banjos”, coupled with “’Bye ’Bye Black Smoke Choo Choo”, with Smith on four-string banjo and Reno on five-string. No chart entry is documented for it, which is precisely why the 1972 title stuck.

The writerComposition

Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith

Smith composed the piece in 1954 by his own account, though Wikipedia’s article on him dates the composition to 1955; the documented recording is April 1955 either way. He is not a background figure here — he wrote it and he recorded it first, and the 1972 film gave him credit for neither.

The creditContested in court

Warner Bros. Records, December 1972

The single named Weissberg alone, omitting even Mandell, and the filmmakers did not credit Smith as composer. He sued Warner Bros. and won a substantial settlement: songwriting credit and back royalties. American Songwriter reports only that the parties settled out of court and that the label credited Smith on later releases; the ABA Journal is the source for the figures — a rejected $15,000 offer, two years, and about $125,000 in legal costs. No source we checked describes a verdict.

Weissberg and Mandell made the famous record. Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith made the first one — and wrote it.

Context

Why the cover swallowed the original

A 1955 banjo instrumental on MGM had almost nothing to carry it beyond the people who already listened to Arthur Smith. No chart entry is documented for the single, and a two-minute novelty instrumental had no obvious second life. The composition’s first genuinely wide airing did not come from the record at all: it came in 1963, when the Darlings — played by the bluegrass group the Dillards — performed it on an episode of The Andy Griffith Show. By the time Deliverance arrived, the tune was folk property in the worst sense: known, unowned in the public mind, and unattached to a name.

The retitling finished the job. A listener who knows it as “Dueling Banjos” has no reason to look for “Feudin’ Banjos”, and the film’s own paperwork encouraged the confusion — the onscreen credit described it as an arrangement of “Feudin’ Banjos” while naming only Weissberg and Mandell, and the December 1972 Warner Bros. single named only Weissberg. So the mistake was not merely popular; it was printed. Smith sued, and every source we checked agrees on how it ended: a settlement, not a verdict. Wikipedia’s Arthur Smith article calls it a “substantial settlement” that awarded him songwriting credit and back royalties, and describes the case as a landmark copyright infringement suit; the Eric Weissberg article uses the same phrase and adds that the settlement appended his film credit. American Songwriter ↗ says the parties settled out of court, the label paying Smith and crediting him on later releases of the album; the ABA Journal ↗ adds the detail, and it is the only source that carries figures — Smith turned down an early offer of $15,000, then spent two years and about $125,000 litigating. The one loose phrasing is in Wikipedia’s Dueling Banjos article, which says only that he “sued and eventually won”; the Arthur Smith article spells out what winning meant here.

The chronology, step by step
  • 1945 — Arthur Smith records “Guitar Boogie”, the instrumental hit that gives him his professional name.
  • 1954 — Smith composes a banjo instrumental he calls “Feudin’ Banjos”. The year is his own account, carried by Wikipedia’s Dueling Banjos article; Wikipedia’s Arthur Smith article puts the composition in 1955, and no documentary source attests 1954.
  • April 1955 — Smith records it for MGM with Don Reno, Smith on four-string banjo and Reno on five-string. It is issued as MGM K12006, coupled with “’Bye ’Bye Black Smoke Choo Choo”. No chart entry is documented.
  • 1963 — The composition gets its first wide-scale airing on The Andy Griffith Show episode “Briscoe Declares for Aunt Bee”, played by the Darlings, portrayed by the Dillards, with Andy Griffith.
  • 1972 — Eric Weissberg and Steve Mandell arrange and record it as “Dueling Banjos” for Deliverance; onscreen it is mimed by Ronny Cox and Billy Redden. The film’s credit calls it an arrangement of “Feudin’ Banjos” but names no composer.
  • December 1972 — Warner Bros. issues the single, credited to Weissberg alone. Mandell’s name is not on it.
  • 1973 — The single reaches No. 2 on the Billboard Hot 100, No. 1 Easy Listening, No. 5 Hot Country and No. 17 in the UK, and is certified Gold. The album, largely a repackage of Weissberg and Marshall Brickman’s 1963 Elektra LP New Dimensions in Banjo and Bluegrass, tops the Billboard 200.
  • 1974 — The recording wins Best Country Instrumental Performance at the 16th Annual Grammy Awards.
  • Eventually — Smith’s action against Warner Bros. ends in a substantial settlement in his favour: songwriting credit and back royalties. He reportedly asked to be left off the film’s credits, having found the film offensive, while wanting his name on the soundtrack release.
The tune took a Grammy in 1974. Its author needed lawyers to get his name on it.
AS

The original recording artist

Arthur “Guitar Boogie” Smith with Don Reno

Arthur Smith 1921-2014 · Don Reno 1926-1984 · MGM Records, 1955

Arthur Smith was born in Clinton, South Carolina in 1921 and came out of the textile-mill South by way of radio, scoring a genuine phenomenon with his 1945 instrumental “Guitar Boogie” — a record credited with selling in the millions and the source of the nickname he carried for life. He was a broadcaster and studio owner as much as a picker: The Arthur Smith Show was the first nationally syndicated country music programme on television, and in 1957 he opened the first commercial recording studio in the Southeast, in Charlotte Wikipedia ↗. In 1954 he wrote a banjo instrumental he called “Feudin’ Banjos”, and in 1955 he cut it for MGM with Don Reno, the Spartanburg-born bluegrass innovator whose single-string picking style made him one of the instrument’s pivotal figures. Smith played the four-string part; Reno answered on five-string. Smith died in Charlotte in 2014, aged 93.

WM

Popularly credited as the original

Eric Weissberg & Steve Mandell

Eric Weissberg 1939-2020 · Steve Mandell c. 1941-2018 · Warner Bros. Records, December 1972

Weissberg was a Brooklyn-born Juilliard student who played with the Greenbriar Boys and the Tarriers before becoming one of New York’s most-called session multi-instrumentalists, turning up behind Bob Dylan, Billy Joel and Barbra Streisand. Mandell was a Philadelphia-born guitarist who came up through the same early-1960s New York folk scene and worked with Judy Collins, Bobby Darin and John Denver. Their arrangement for Deliverance is a marvellous piece of playing — the two instruments circling, goading and finally sprinting — and it deserved every bit of its No. 2 Hot 100 placing and its Grammy. The only thing wrong with the popular account is the chronology, and the credit line, neither of which was their doing: Weissberg himself blamed the label, and Mandell’s name was left off the single entirely.
