Who recorded it first

Don't Leave Me This Way

Not a Communards original — a 1975 Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes album track

Ask most people in Britain who did “Don’t Leave Me This Way” and the answer is The Communards, whose 1986 version spent four weeks at UK number one and outsold every other single that year. It is a cover, and so is the other famous version. The song was written by Kenneth Gamble, Leon Huff and Cary Gilbert, and first recorded by Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes with Teddy Pendergrass singing lead, for their November 1975 Philadelphia International album Wake Up Everybody ↗. It was never issued as a US single at the time and never touched the American pop or R&B charts — it simply sat on side two while two other singers took it to number one.

How the chronology checks out

The reference article on the song states plainly that it was first released in 1975 by Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes on Philadelphia International, that it was not issued as a US single at the time, and that it still reached number three on the Billboard Hot Disco Singles chart — Don’t Leave Me This Way ↗.

The album’s own entry confirms the physical facts: Wake Up Everybody, Philadelphia International, 1975, produced by Gamble and Huff, with “Don’t Leave Me This Way” credited to Gamble, Huff and Cary Gilbert at 6:08 — Wake Up Everybody ↗.

The strongest check against an earlier version is the composition’s catalogue entry, which lists more than three hundred recordings of the song. Sorted by first release date, the Blue Notes recording sits at the top and nothing predates it; Thelma Houston’s appears in 1976 and The Communards’ in 1986 — MusicBrainz work entry ↗.

The structured record independently names Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes as the performer and dates first publication to November 1975 — Wikidata Q3713449 ↗.

Fact-check

What actually happened

This is an unusually clean case: the song had two enormous hit versions and neither act recorded it first. In Britain the credit almost always lands on The Communards; in America it lands on Thelma Houston. Both are covers of a Philadelphia soul album track.

The beliefCover

The Communards, 1986

Their London Records version spent four weeks at UK number one and was the best-selling British single of 1986, which is why it is remembered as the definitive — and often the first — recording.

The other claimantAlso a cover

Thelma Houston, 1976

Her Tamla version topped the Billboard Hot 100 in April 1977 and won a Grammy, so in the United States she is the one usually taken for the originator. The song had first been assigned to Diana Ross before being passed to Houston.

The originalFirst recording

Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes, November 1975

Cut at Sigma Sound in Philadelphia for Philadelphia International, with Teddy Pendergrass on lead and Gamble and Huff producing, and released as track four, the opening track on side two of Wake Up Everybody. It was not issued as a US single at the time, which is precisely why nobody remembers it as first.

The writersComposition

Kenneth Gamble, Leon Huff and Cary Gilbert

None of the three performers wrote it. The song belongs to the Philadelphia International writing team, with Gilbert — who also co-wrote “Me and Mrs. Jones” — the least-known of the three names on the label.

First recorded by Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes, November 1975, Philadelphia International. Everything after that is a cover.

Context

Why the cover ate the original

The 1975 recording is not a lesser version of the song people know — it is a different song in temperament. Gamble and Huff produced it as a long, unhurried soul ballad with Teddy Pendergrass pleading over the top, and Philadelphia International left it on the album rather than pressing it as a US single. That decision is the whole story. A track nobody could buy on a 45 could not chart, could not get pop radio, and could not lodge itself in anyone’s memory as the definitive take.

What happened instead is that other producers heard what the song could become at a faster tempo. Motown’s Hal Davis heard the Blue Notes recording at a party while making Thelma Houston’s album Any Way You Like It ↗, and rebuilt it as a disco record; Houston took it to number one on the Hot 100 and won a Grammy. A decade on, The Communards did the same trick again with hi-NRG synthesisers and a four-week UK number one. Both of the versions everyone remembers are reinterpretations of a ballad that was never given a chance to be a hit on its own terms.

The chronology, step by step
  • November 1975 — Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes release Wake Up Everybody on Philadelphia International, produced by Kenneth Gamble and Leon Huff. “Don’t Leave Me This Way”, written by Gamble, Huff and Cary Gilbert, is track four, the opening track on side two, with Teddy Pendergrass on lead vocal. It is not issued as a US single.
  • 1976 — the song is first assigned to Diana Ross at Motown as the follow-up to “Love Hangover”, then given instead to Thelma Houston, who records her vocal at Motown’s West Hollywood studio with producer Hal Davis.
  • December 1976 — Houston’s version is released on Tamla. It reaches number one on the Billboard Hot 100 in April 1977 and number one on the soul singles chart, and wins the Grammy for Best R&B Vocal Performance, Female.
  • 1977 — in the wake of Houston’s hit, the Blue Notes album cut reaches number three on the US Billboard disco chart, despite never having had a US single release. It does not appear on the US pop or R&B charts.
  • 1977 — the Blue Notes recording is finally issued as a single in the United Kingdom and reaches number five. A US 12-inch follows in 1979.
  • August 1986 — The Communards, with guest vocalist Sarah Jane Morris, release their version on London Records. It spends four weeks at UK number one and becomes the best-selling British single of the year.
Two number ones, and neither act cut it first.
HM

The original recording artist

Harold Melvin & the Blue Notes

American soul group, formed in Philadelphia in 1954

The Blue Notes formed in Philadelphia in the mid-1950s and spent nearly two decades as a working vocal group before signing to Gamble and Huff’s Philadelphia International Records in 1972 and becoming one of the defining acts of Philly soul. Their lead singer from 1970 was Teddy Pendergrass, who had joined as a drummer and moved to the front of the group when John Atkins left. It is Pendergrass’s voice on the 1975 recording of “Don’t Leave Me This Way” — a slow, aching six-minute performance rather than a dance record. He quit the group in 1976, the same year the song began its second life elsewhere; Harold Melvin died in 1997.

TC

Popularly credited as the original

The Communards

British synth-pop duo, active 1985 to 1988

Jimmy Somerville and Richard Coles recorded together as The Communards between 1985 and 1988, pairing Somerville’s falsetto with Coles’s keyboards and a hi-NRG pulse. Their 1986 reading of “Don’t Leave Me This Way”, with guest vocalist Sarah Jane Morris answering Somerville in a startlingly deep contralto, went to number one in the UK for four weeks and finished the year as the country’s biggest-selling single. It is a genuinely inventive arrangement and the reason a whole generation knows the song at all — the only thing wrong with the popular credit is the date.

GH

The songwriters

Kenneth Gamble, Leon Huff & Cary Gilbert

Philadelphia International songwriting team, 1970s

Kenneth Gamble and Leon Huff built Philadelphia International Records and wrote and produced much of the catalogue that became known as the Philly sound. Cary Gilbert, born in 1942 and known to friends as Hippy, grew up in Camden, New Jersey and collaborated with the pair through the 1970s; the two songs he is best remembered for are “Me and Mrs. Jones” for Billy Paul and this one. Gamble and Huff also produced the 1975 Blue Notes recording. Gilbert died in February 1993, aged 50.
