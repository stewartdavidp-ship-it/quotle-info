Who recorded it first

Babe I'm Gonna Leave You

Not a Led Zeppelin original — Joan Baez recorded it first, in 1962

Because it opens Led Zeppelin’s 1969 debut, the song is widely taken to be theirs from the ground up. It isn’t: the first recording to reach the public was Joan Baez’s, the opening track of her live album Joan Baez in Concert, released by Vanguard Records in September 1962 — and it was Baez’s record that put the song in front of Jimmy Page. The writer was neither of them. Anne Bredon composed it as a student at Berkeley in the late 1950s, but Baez released it as “traditional, arr. Baez” and Led Zeppelin as “Traditional, arr. Jimmy Page,” so Bredon’s name was missing from the credits for decades until she pursued the band and won both a writing credit and a substantial back-payment of royalties.

How a Berkeley student’s song became a Led Zeppelin opener

Anne Bredon wrote the song while a student at UC Berkeley in the late 1950s and sang it around 1960 on The Midnight Special, a live folk show on Berkeley radio station KPFA — per Wikipedia’s biography of Bredon ↗.

The folk singer Janet Smith heard that broadcast and carried the song east; Baez learned it from her at Oberlin College, and recorded it not knowing who had written it — see Wikipedia on the song ↗.

Vanguard released Joan Baez in Concert in September 1962 with the song as its opening track, credited only as traditional; the album reached No. 10 on the Billboard Pop Albums chart, per Wikipedia’s album entry ↗.

Jimmy Page found the song on Baez’s record and worked it up with Robert Plant in August 1968; it opens the band’s Atlantic debut ↗, originally credited “Traditional, arranged Jimmy Page.”

Fact-check

Who actually got there first

This is a case where the famous record is not the first record, and where the person who wrote the song was absent from both sleeves.

The beliefcover, 1969

Led Zeppelin

It opens their 1969 debut on Atlantic and is one of their signature performances, so it reads as an original — and early pressings said “Traditional, arranged Jimmy Page,” which encouraged exactly that reading.

The originalfirst released recording, 1962

Joan Baez

Her solo live performance opens Joan Baez in Concert, released by Vanguard Records in September 1962. It is the recording Jimmy Page heard.

The writercomposer, late 1950s

Anne Bredon

She wrote it as a Berkeley student and sang it on KPFA radio around 1960. Neither the Baez release nor the Led Zeppelin release named her at the time.

Written by Anne Bredon in the late 1950s, first put on record by Joan Baez in 1962, and made famous — not made — by Led Zeppelin in 1969.

Context

Why the cover eclipsed the original

The interesting question is not why Led Zeppelin’s version is better known — a loud British rock band on Atlantic in 1969 will always out-travel one woman with a guitar on a folk label. It is why the song arrived at both artists with no author attached. It moved the way folk songs move: Bredon sang it on Berkeley community radio, a listener learned it, carried it across the country, taught it to Joan Baez, and by the time Baez recorded it nobody in the chain could say where it came from. So it was labelled traditional, which is what the folk revival called a song whose parent it had lost.

That label is what made the misattribution durable. Baez’s sleeve said traditional; Led Zeppelin’s said traditional, arranged by Jimmy Page — and an arrangement credit on a traditional song is a claim of authorship in all but name. Bredon, who had left performing behind, did not find out until the 1980s. Baez had in fact printed Bredon’s name in her 1964 songbook; the correction simply never reached the record that mattered.

The chronology, step by step
  • Late 1950s — Anne Bredon writes the song while studying at UC Berkeley.
  • Around 1960 — she performs it on The Midnight Special, a live folk programme on Berkeley radio station KPFA. Singer Janet Smith hears it and takes up the song.
  • Joan Baez learns it from Smith at Oberlin College, without knowing who wrote it.
  • September 1962 — Vanguard Records releases Joan Baez in Concert (VSD-2122) with the song as its opening track, credited as traditional, arranged by Baez. The album reaches No. 10 on the Billboard Pop Albums chart.
  • 1964 — Bredon is named as the author in The Joan Baez Song Book.
  • August 1968 — Jimmy Page, who knows the song from Baez’s record, works on it with Robert Plant at their first meeting.
  • September and October 1968 — Led Zeppelin record their version at Olympic Studios, London.
  • January 1969 — the song opens Led Zeppelin on Atlantic Records, credited “Traditional, arranged Jimmy Page.”
  • 1980s — Bredon learns of the Led Zeppelin recording and takes the matter up with the band.
  • Since the early 1990s — the Led Zeppelin version is credited to Anne Bredon, Jimmy Page and Robert Plant, and Bredon received a substantial back-payment of royalties.
It was never traditional. It just travelled like it was.
JB

The original recording artist

Joan Baez

b. 9 January 1941, Staten Island, New York · American folk singer

Baez began recording in 1960 and was, within two years, a defining voice of the American folk revival — a soprano of unusual clarity, her own accompanist, and an activist who made protest and social justice part of the repertoire. Her live album Joan Baez in Concert, taped on her 1962 US tour and released by Vanguard that September, opens with this song. She had learned it from another singer and did not know who wrote it, so it went out as traditional — an honest gap she filled two years later in her own songbook.

LZ

Popularly credited as the original

Led Zeppelin

Formed 1968, London · English rock band

Page knew the song from Baez’s record and brought it to his first sessions with Robert Plant in 1968; the arrangement cut at Olympic Studios that autumn swings between Page’s acoustic Gibson J-200 and his electric Telecaster, and is one of the most admired things on the band’s debut. The performance is genuinely theirs — the dynamics, the structure, the ferocity are all new. The only errors were chronological and clerical: the sleeve said “Traditional, arranged Jimmy Page” when the song had a living author.

AB

The songwriter

Anne Bredon

7 September 1930 – 9 November 2019 · American folk singer and composer

Born Anne Loeb into a family of scientists, Bredon studied art at Humboldt State and took a master’s degree in mathematics at UC Berkeley, where she wrote this song in the late 1950s. She sang it on KPFA around 1960 and then largely left music behind; the song travelled without her. Only in the 1980s did she learn that Led Zeppelin had recorded it. She pursued the matter, and their version has since been credited to Bredon, Page and Plant, with a substantial back-payment of royalties.
