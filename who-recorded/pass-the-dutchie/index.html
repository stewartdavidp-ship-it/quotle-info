Who recorded it first

Pass the Dutchie

Not a Musical Youth original — a 1981 Mighty Diamonds single, “Pass the Kouchie”

Musical Youth’s 1982 record is so completely theirs in memory that most people assume they made it up. They didn’t: the song was first recorded in 1981 by the Mighty Diamonds as “Pass the Kouchie”, produced by Augustus “Gussie” Clarke for the Music Works label and carried on the album Changes. The composition credits run to Diamonds members Fitzroy “Bunny” Simpson and Lloyd “Judge” Ferguson alongside Jackie Mittoo, whose rocksteady instrumental “Full Up” supplies the rhythm — though Wikipedia’s source credits “Full Up” to Leroy Sibbles rather than Mittoo, and both men appear on the composition’s writer list. The Jamaican original was condemned by Prime Minister Edward Seaga and banned from national radio — and a 1984 settlement agreement, recited in the 2012 English High Court judgment, deemed the copyrights in “Kouchie” and “Dutchie” to derive from two equal contributions.

Fact-check

Right record, wrong origin story

Nobody disputes that Musical Youth made the hit. What gets lost is that they were covering a Jamaican single that was barely a year old at the time — and that a court eventually said so in as many words.

The beliefthe famous version

Musical Youth, 1982

Their MCA single reached No. 1 in the UK and No. 10 in the US, and its MTV airplay made it one of the most-seen reggae records of the decade. It is a cover with rewritten lyrics, not an original.

The originalfirst recording

The Mighty Diamonds, 1981

Released as “Pass the Kouchie” on Gussie Clarke’s Music Works label and included on the album Changes. Banned from Jamaican national radio after Prime Minister Edward Seaga condemned it.

The writerscomposition

Fitzroy “Bunny” Simpson, Lloyd “Judge” Ferguson and Jackie Mittoo

Two Mighty Diamonds plus the rocksteady keyboardist whose instrumental “Full Up” underpins the rhythm. MusicBrainz’s entry for the composition adds Leroy Sibbles, U Brown, Robbie Lyn and “Deadly” Headley Bennett to the credit list.

The rulingsettled in law

The English High Court, 2012

In Seaton & Ors v Seddon & Ors, Mr Justice Roth records that “Pass the Dutchie” is an adaptation of “Pass the Kouchie” and sets out clause 1 of the 1984 Sparta Florida settlement agreement, under which the copyrights of the two songs were to be treated as deriving from two equal contributions. The former Musical Youth members’ claim against their old solicitors was dismissed as time-barred, without the copyright question being decided.

First recorded by the Mighty Diamonds in 1981, as “Pass the Kouchie”.

Context

Why the cover swallowed the original

The interesting thing here is that the eclipse was engineered. The Mighty Diamonds’ record was about Rastafarian cannabis use and said so plainly; a “kouchie” is the pipe. When the song was rebuilt for the British market in 1982, the record company had the drug references taken out — the title became “dutchie”, a patois word for a Dutch oven, and the song was reframed around food. A record designed for radio replaced a record that had been banned from it. Wikipedia notes the further irony that “dutchie” itself later drifted into cannabis slang anyway.

The other reason is arithmetic of reach. The Jamaican original was a Music Works pressing in a national market with the state broadcaster closed to it; the adaptation had MCA, a Don Letts video and MTV in its first year of existence. Wikipedia records that the British version also folded in U Brown’s “Gimme the Music”, so what most listeners hear as one catchy novelty is in fact several Jamaican records stacked on top of Jackie Mittoo’s rhythm.

The chronology, step by step
  • 1968 — the rocksteady instrumental “Full Up” is cut in Kingston; the 2012 High Court judgment attributes the music to Jackie Mittoo, and Wikipedia, citing musicologist George Lipsitz, credits Leroy Sibbles. Both men appear on the composition’s writer list.
  • 1981 — the Mighty Diamonds record “Pass the Kouchie” for Augustus “Gussie” Clarke’s Music Works label; it appears on the album Changes.
  • 1981 — Prime Minister Edward Seaga condemns the record and it is banned from Jamaican national radio. It sells heavily in record shops regardless.
  • 1982 — Musical Youth record an adaptation in August with the drug references removed. MCA releases “Pass the Dutchie” that September — Wikipedia dates it 10 September, the 2012 judgment 26 September — and it tops the UK chart.
  • February 1983 — the single peaks at No. 10 on the US Billboard Hot 100; the video has already become the first by a black act shown on MTV.
  • 23 March 2012 — in Seaton & Ors v Seddon & Ors, Mr Justice Roth dismisses the former band members’ claim against their former solicitors as time-barred. The judgment decides nothing about the copyright itself: it recites the 1984 Sparta Florida agreement, under which the copyrights of “Kouchie” and “Dutchie” were deemed to derive from two equal contributions. The flatter claim that the 2012 case made the two songs share one copyright is Wikipedia’s summary, not the court’s holding.

MD

The original recording artist

The Mighty Diamonds

Roots reggae vocal trio, formed 1969, Trenchtown, Kingston

Donald “Tabby” Shaw sang lead, with Fitzroy “Bunny” Simpson and Lloyd “Judge” Ferguson in harmony — a Motown-shaped vocal group working in the roots idiom, and by the mid-1970s one of the most respected acts in Jamaica on the strength of Right Time. “Pass the Kouchie” arrived in 1981 on Gussie Clarke’s Music Works label and promptly got them denounced by the Prime Minister and banned from national radio, which did nothing to slow it in the record shops. Simpson and Ferguson are two of the three writers credited on the record label (with Jackie Mittoo); the composition’s full credit list runs to seven. Shaw was killed in a drive-by shooting in March 2022; Simpson died days later.

MY

Popularly credited as the original

Musical Youth

British reggae group, formed 1979, Birmingham, England

Two pairs of brothers — Kelvin and Michael Grant, Frederick “Junior” and Patrick Waite — assembled by their fathers in 1979 while still at Duddeston Manor School in Birmingham. By the MCA signing Dennis Seaton had taken over lead vocals from Frederick Waite Jr., and it is Seaton’s name at the head of the 2012 case. Their MCA single went to No. 1 in the UK and No. 10 in the US, sold in the millions, and its Don Letts–directed video became the first by a black act to air on MTV. None of that is diminished by the chronology: it is a superb record made by children who were handed someone else’s song and a set of rewritten words, and the only error in the popular memory is the word “original”.
