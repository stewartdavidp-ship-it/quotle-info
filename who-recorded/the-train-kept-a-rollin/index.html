Who recorded it first

The Train Kept A-Rollin'

Not an Aerosmith original — a 1951 Tiny Bradshaw single on King Records

Classic-rock radio has played Aerosmith’s version so long that most listeners file the song under Aerosmith, who cut it for “Get Your Wings” in 1974. It was first recorded by bandleader Tiny Bradshaw and his orchestra in Cincinnati on 6 October 1951, and issued by King Records that December as a jump-blues single paired with “Knockin’ Blues.” The composition is credited to Bradshaw with Howie Kay and “Lois Mann” — the latter a pen name used by King’s owner Syd Nathan. Bradshaw’s record never reached the national R&B charts; the song only became famous through a chain of later covers.

Fact-check

Setting the record straight

Aerosmith’s 1974 recording is the one most people have heard, and for many listeners it is simply where the song comes from. The paperwork says otherwise.

The beliefCover, 1974

Aerosmith

Their two-part hard rock version appeared on “Get Your Wings” (Columbia) in March 1974 and became a classic-rock staple. The October 1974 single edit did not chart. They were working from the Yardbirds’ 1965 arrangement, not from scratch.

The originalFirst recording, 1951

Tiny Bradshaw and his orchestra

Recorded in Cincinnati on 6 October 1951 and released by King Records that December on 78 and 45 (catalogue 45-4497), paired with “Knockin’ Blues.” A jump-blues record with horns and shuffle rhythm. It did not appear on the national R&B charts.

“Lois Mann” was a pen name used by King Records owner Syd Nathan — a label-boss share of the publishing rather than a contribution to the song. Bradshaw derived the piece from the earlier “Cow-Cow Boogie” (1942).

Context

How a jump-blues B-list single became an Aerosmith song

The song did not travel from Bradshaw to Aerosmith in one leap — it was handed along. Johnny Burnette and the Rock ‘n’ Roll Trio recorded it on 2 July 1956 in Nashville and Coral released it that September, and guitarist Paul Burlison’s deliberately distorted tone on that record is often cited as one of the first intentional uses of guitar distortion on a rock recording. The Yardbirds cut their version in September 1965 and released it that November on Epic; Jeff Beck’s fuzz-tone arrangement became the template everyone afterwards followed, and Led Zeppelin later used it as a concert opener.

By the time Aerosmith reached it in 1974, the song had been a rock song for eighteen years and a jump-blues record for less than five. The scale of that eclipse is captured by a remark Jeff Beck reported hearing from listeners — that they liked his angle on the Aerosmith tune. If the man whose arrangement Aerosmith were working from got mistaken for the imitator, a 1951 Cincinnati bandleader never stood a chance.

The chain of custody, step by step
  • 6 October 1951 — Tiny Bradshaw and his orchestra record the song in Cincinnati, Ohio.
  • December 1951 — King Records issues it on 10-inch 78 and 7-inch 45 (catalogue 45-4497), paired with “Knockin’ Blues.” The record does not reach the national R&B charts.
  • 23 February 1952 — Billboard’s Rhythm & Blues Record Reviews column praises the vocal on what it calls a rocking novelty.
  • September 1956 — Johnny Burnette and the Rock ‘n’ Roll Trio release a rockabilly version on Coral, with Paul Burlison’s distorted guitar.
  • September–November 1965 — The Yardbirds record and release their fuzz-tone arrangement on Epic; it becomes the version later bands copy.
  • March 1974 — Aerosmith release their two-part hard rock version on “Get Your Wings” (Columbia). A single edit follows in October 1974 and does not chart.
Bradshaw’s record never charted. Neither did anyone else’s — the song outlived the charts entirely.
TB

The original recording artist

Tiny Bradshaw

1907-1958 · American bandleader, singer, pianist and drummer

Myron “Tiny” Bradshaw was born in Youngstown, Ohio in 1907 and led his own swing orchestra from 1934. Signed to Cincinnati’s King Records, he placed five records on the Billboard R&B chart between late 1949 and early 1955, including the number-two hit “Well Oh Well.” His band was a finishing school for players such as tenor saxophonist Red Prysock, who blows on the 1951 session. A stroke in 1954 curtailed his career, and he died in Cincinnati in 1958 at fifty-one, long before the song he cut became a rock standard.

AE

Popularly credited as the original

Aerosmith

Formed 1970 in Boston · American hard rock band

Aerosmith recorded the song at the Record Plant in New York between December 1973 and January 1974 and released it on their second album, “Get Your Wings,” on Columbia in March 1974. Their arrangement is in two distinct parts — a slow, groove-heavy opening that erupts into a full-tilt rocker — with lead guitar contributions from session players Steve Hunter and Dick Wagner. An edited single followed in October 1974 and did not chart, but the album version became a fixture of album-oriented rock radio and remains one of the band’s best-known performances. The record is superb; only the assumption that they wrote or first cut it is wrong.

BK

The songwriters

Tiny Bradshaw, Howie Kay and Lois Mann

Credited on the 1951 King Records single and in the MusicBrainz work entry

The composition is registered to three names: Tiny Bradshaw, Howie Kay and Lois Mann. Bradshaw built the song by reworking the earlier “Cow-Cow Boogie” (1942) into an urban, hipster train scenario. “Lois Mann” was a pen name used by King Records owner Syd Nathan, a common label-owner device for taking a cut of publishing income; historian Larry Birnbaum has written that Kay’s identity remains obscure. The original King 45 label itself carried only a Bradshaw–Mann credit.
