Who recorded it first

The House of the Rising Sun

Not an Animals original — a 1933 Vocalion 78 by Clarence “Tom” Ashley and Gwen Foster

Ask most people who wrote or first recorded “The House of the Rising Sun” and the answer is The Animals, whose 1964 single reached No. 1 in both Britain and America. But the oldest known recording of the song is “Rising Sun Blues,” cut on 6 September 1933 by Clarence “Tom” Ashley and Gwen Foster ↗ and issued on Vocalion 02576. Nobody wrote it: it is a traditional song of uncertain authorship, catalogued as Roud 6393, and Ashley said he learned it from his grandfather Enoch Ashley. The 78 sold into the Depression-era hillbilly market and vanished; it was the folk revival, not the record shop, that carried the song forward.

Fact-check

The record, corrected

The Animals’ 1964 single is so complete a performance that it reads as the song’s point of origin. It is not, and the gap is not small.

The beliefPopular credit

The Animals, 1964

Their single, recorded 18 May 1964 and released by Columbia on 19 June, hit No. 1 in Britain and No. 1 in America for three weeks. Most listeners meet the song here and assume it began here.

The originalOldest known recording

Clarence “Tom” Ashley and Gwen Foster, 1933

They recorded it as “Rising Sun Blues” on 6 September 1933 for Vocalion, issued as catalogue 02576 with “East Virginia Blues” on the reverse. Wikipedia describes it as the oldest known recording of the song.

The writerRoud 6393

Traditional; author unknown

No individual wrote it. It is a traditional song of uncertain authorship, indexed as Roud 6393, and Ashley said he had it from his grandfather — which puts it well before any recording.

The arrangementThe missing link

Dave Van Ronk, by way of Bob Dylan

Van Ronk gave the song its half-step descending bass line in the 1950s. Dylan recorded that arrangement on his 1962 debut, and it is that version of the song, not the 1933 78, that the Animals were working from.

The Animals made the definitive recording of a song that was already more than thirty years old on record and far older than that in the singing.

Context

Why the 1964 record swallowed the song

The Animals did not learn this song from a 78. They learned it from the folk revival, which had been passing it hand to hand for decades. Alan Lomax recorded a sixteen-year-old Kentucky miner’s daughter, Georgia Turner, singing it in Middlesboro and called it “The Rising Sun Blues.” Roy Acuff cut a version in 1938, the Almanac Singers in 1941, Josh White and Libby Holman in 1942, Lead Belly in the 1940s, Pete Seeger and Joan Baez by the end of the 1950s.

The decisive link is Dave Van Ronk, who picked the song up in the 1950s from a Hally Wood recording and rebuilt it around a bass line descending in half steps. Bob Dylan recorded Van Ronk’s arrangement for his 1962 debut album — to Van Ronk’s lasting irritation — and it was that shape of the song, not Ashley and Foster’s, that reached a Newcastle beat group two years later. What the Animals originated was an arrangement. What they inherited was a ballad older than the recording industry.

The chain of custody, step by step
  • 6 September 1933 — Clarence “Tom” Ashley and Gwen Foster record “Rising Sun Blues”; Vocalion issues it as 02576, backed with “East Virginia Blues.”
  • 1937 — Alan Lomax records Georgia Turner, a miner’s daughter in Middlesboro, Kentucky, and titles her version “The Rising Sun Blues.”
  • 1938-1948 — Roy Acuff (1938), the Almanac Singers (1941), Josh White and Libby Holman (1942) and Lead Belly all record versions.
  • 1950s — Dave Van Ronk learns the song from a Hally Wood recording and gives it a bass line descending in half steps.
  • 1962 — Bob Dylan records Van Ronk’s arrangement for his debut album.
  • 18 May 1964 — The Animals record their version in a single take, produced by Mickie Most.
  • 19 June 1964 — Columbia releases the single in the UK; MGM 13264 follows in the US. It tops both charts.
The song has no author to steal from — only a long line of people who passed it on.
AF

The original recording artists

Clarence “Tom” Ashley & Gwen Foster

Ashley 1895-1967, banjo and guitar; Foster 1903-1954, harmonica and guitar

Clarence Earl McCurry, who performed as Clarence “Tom” Ashley, was born in Bristol, Tennessee in 1895 and went out with medicine shows from around 1911, playing clawhammer banjo in a “sawmill” tuning he picked up from family. Gwen Foster, a Caldwell County, North Carolina harmonica and guitar player who had worked the textile mills alongside Dock Walsh of the Carolina Tar Heels, partnered him on record. Their “Rising Sun Blues” for Vocalion in September 1933 is the oldest known recording of the song. Ashley was rediscovered during the 1960s folk revival, thanks partly to Harry Smith’s Anthology of American Folk Music, and played Newport and Carnegie Hall with Doc Watson before his death in 1967.

TA

Popularly credited as the original

The Animals

Newcastle beat group; single produced by Mickie Most

The Animals recorded their arrangement in a single take on 18 May 1964 and Columbia released it in Britain on 19 June. It went to No. 1 in the UK, then to No. 1 in the United States on MGM, where it held the top spot for three weeks. The record deserves its reputation: Eric Burdon’s vocal and Alan Price’s organ turned a mountain ballad into something enormous, and it is routinely described as one of the first folk-rock hits. The only mistake anyone makes about it is chronological.

TR

The songwriter

Traditional

Author unknown; Roud Folk Song Index 6393

There is no author to name. The song is traditional, of uncertain authorship, and carries the number 6393 in the Roud Folk Song Index. Its lineage is usually traced to the British broadside ballad tradition, with one theory holding that it travelled from England to the American South; another reads the “rising sun” as a sunburst insignia dating to the reign of Louis XIV. Ashley’s own account — that he had it from his grandfather — puts the song comfortably back into the nineteenth century.
